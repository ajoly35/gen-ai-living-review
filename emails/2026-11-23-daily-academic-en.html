Gen-AI, Jobs and Growth

23 November 2026 · One new study on AI and work, checked against its full text.

Borri et al. (2026), "The Rise of Agents and the Labor Market"

Luiss, Dallas Fed, Rochester and Yale, arXiv preprint, 20 November 2026. Paper. Stock-market exposure to AI agent use and job postings of 1,784 listed US firms; event study around Claude Code 2.0 (September 2025).

What it finds. Once AI agents improved, more exposed firms advertised fewer jobs, mostly junior ones. The study tracks 34.8 million Lightcast ads from January 2022 to July 2026, measuring exposure by how a firm's stock return moves with weekly agentic token use on OpenRouter (about 2% of global AI tokens). After Claude Code 2.0 (29 September 2025), firms one standard deviation more exposed beforehand posted 4.73% lower six-month ad growth than less exposed firms, despite showing no gap before; the panel estimate is 2.11%. Non-senior postings fell 4.54%, while senior postings showed no significant change. Advertised wages held steady, and broad AI exposure predicts nothing once agentic exposure is included.

Why it matters. This paper brings agents into section “Exposure designs: hiring and entry”. As in Hosseini and Lichtinger (2025) and Ahn and Carollo (2026), adjustment comes through fewer junior hires. Flat advertised wages stand in contrast to the pay cuts in Azar et al. (2026). Once firm exposure is known, occupation scores (Eloundou et al. 2024) add little, in line with Massenkoff and McCrory (2026): exposure is not use.

Caveat. Because exposure is inferred from stock prices, it may also pick up expected demand shocks. Job postings are not hires, the sample covers only listed firms, and OpenRouter traffic may not reflect how large companies use agents internally.

Van Nieuwerburgh and Su (2026), "Clouds of Credit: Mapping the Financial Web Behind AI Compute"

Columbia Business School, NBER Working Paper 36032, November 2026. Paper. Network model of NVIDIA, six hyperscalers, two AI labs, two neoclouds and data-centre landlords, calibrated to their claims at 30 June 2026; theory, not a causal design.

What it finds. Firms build too much AI compute, and debt leaves the network fragile. In equilibrium, the network adds 277.5 GW of compute for $6.30 trillion in capital spending. That is 55% above the planner's 178.9 GW, because each firm ignores how its own capacity cuts rivals' revenue. Debt pays for about 59% of the expansion, and lender debt rises from 5.9% to 26.3% of equity. If AI productivity turns out very low, a state given a 25% probability, outside investors lose $16.87 trillion, compared with $8.8 trillion without the buildout, while outside lenders lose about 42% of their stake. Blocking payment spillovers between firms cuts these losses by about 30%.

Why it matters. For the companion review's section on aggregating task gains: scenarios like Korinek et al. (2026) take the compute path as given, whereas here competing firms choose it and overshoot. The paper calibrates the AI investment race of Rungcharoenkitkul (2026) on measured contracts and extends Van Nieuwerburgh (2026). It maps the downside of the buildout rather than offering a growth forecast.

Caveat. The productivity states, their probabilities and many private-firm exposures rest on assumptions. Because market values discipline the calibration, any mispricing feeds straight into the results. The model also has just one period, with no feedback to the wider economy.

Main ideas

  • Large gains on tasks, none yet in the aggregates. In field experiments, generative AI raises output on narrow tasks by 14 to 34%, most for workers who were least productive before. In the Nordic registers, total employment, hours and earnings are unchanged so far.
  • The adjustment runs through entry, not layoffs. Young hires in the most exposed occupations have fallen in the US, Sweden, the UK and France, by up to a quarter relative to less exposed occupations, and contract ends fell too: firms stop hiring juniors rather than dismiss workers. Hires are a small flow, so the stock of jobs moves slowly and totals look flat.
  • Exposed occupations adjust through pay as well. Wages in the most exposed occupations fell by about 5% by 2026 with no change in headcount, and this explains close to a third of the fall in the college wage premium.
  • Firm-level results split. Some adopters cut junior employment by about 9% while senior employment is unchanged; others, especially the biggest spenders on AI, grew and hired more entrants. Who counts as an adopter drives much of the gap, and whether substitution or scale wins is the central open question.
  • Juniors lose because their tasks are the most codifiable. AI raises novices' output most, but hiring depends on demand for the firm's output, not on output per worker. If AI does the routine work juniors used to train on, firms have less reason to hire and train them.
  • Organisation decides the direction. Management quality, the cost of reorganising and the flexibility of labour law set how deeply firms adopt. So far AI mostly saves time within existing jobs and moves knowledge down the hierarchy; new tasks appear only where employers reorganise.
  • What is still unknown. How strongly demand responds to cheaper output, how much of the fall in entry is AI rather than remote work or a slowdown that began in 2022, where the fall in entry ends as models improve, and who captures the gains.
