Gen-AI, Jobs and Growth

20 November 2026 · One new study on AI and work, checked against its full text.

Chow et al. (2026), "AI-Induced Social Erosion: Using LLMs for Work Tasks Reduces Help-Seeking Intentions Towards Colleagues"

NYU, Illinois and Carnegie Mellon, arXiv preprint, 18 November 2026. Paper. Randomised vignette experiment on 248 US knowledge workers; outcomes are stated intentions, not observed behaviour.

What it finds. Workers who pictured doing a task with AI were less inclined to ask a colleague for help. In the study, 248 US knowledge workers who use AI weekly rated ten hypothetical scenarios each (2,444 ratings) on 1 to 5 scales. Compared with doing the same tasks without AI, using AI lowered help-seeking by 0.24 points and felt connection to the colleague by 0.25 points. The fall was sharpest for brainstorming (0.41 points) and proofing (0.26), and not significant for reassurance and search. Using AI in a workspace colleagues can see, rather than in private, raised help-seeking by 0.31 points. Delegating the task to AI instead of being assisted by it made no difference.

Why it matters. This speaks to section “Who holds knowledge?”: if AI answers the questions workers used to bring to colleagues, it replaces the help layer of the Garicano (2000) hierarchy, as in Ide and Talamàs (2025). That contrasts with Soda, Iorio and Rizzo (2026), where Copilot adopters collaborated with more colleagues. Still, the shared-workspace effect backs the review's point that organisation decides the direction.

Caveat. Hypothetical scenarios capture intentions on the day, not behaviour over months. The sample consists of self-selected online workers who already trust AI, and the effects are a fraction of a point on a five-point scale.

Dueñas and Mandel (2026), "Structural economic change under the digital transformation"

Sant'Anna Pisa and Paris School of Economics, arXiv preprint, 19 November 2026. Paper. Agent-based model of 163 industries in 49 economies, calibrated to 2022 input-output data; simulation, not a causal design.

What it finds. If digital suppliers (computing, telecoms, electronics and ICT services) become 35% more productive over ten years, GDP rises everywhere, by about 3% in the EU and close to 6% in China. Gross output grows more than value added, and value added falls in some sectors, such as agriculture and food in Mexico, India and Canada. When digital inputs directly replace capital and labour in the 49 industries that OECD taxonomies rank most AI- or digital-exposed, digital value added rises by 48% in the UK to just over 70% in China. Workers shift into digital industries, mostly from services. Without labour mobility, reallocation gaps reach 1.0 to 1.3% of employment in Germany and 0.5 to 0.7% in the US.

Why it matters. For the companion review's section on aggregating task gains, this study adds propagation through production networks. Gains show up more in gross output than in value added, a channel that Acemoglu (2025) and Korinek et al. (2026) leave out. Lasting wage gaps across industries also shape whether displaced workers can move, as in Hyman et al. (2025).

Caveat. The study assumes the productivity shock rather than estimating it, and covers all digital suppliers rather than AI alone. Substitution elasticities are set rather than measured, and the labour gaps reflect wage-based pressures, not unemployment.

Main ideas

  • Large gains on tasks, none yet in the aggregates. In field experiments, generative AI raises output on narrow tasks by 14 to 34%, most for workers who were least productive before. In the Nordic registers, total employment, hours and earnings are unchanged so far.
  • The adjustment runs through entry, not layoffs. Young hires in the most exposed occupations have fallen in the US, Sweden, the UK and France, by up to a quarter relative to less exposed occupations, and contract ends fell too: firms stop hiring juniors rather than dismiss workers. Hires are a small flow, so the stock of jobs moves slowly and totals look flat.
  • Exposed occupations adjust through pay as well. Wages in the most exposed occupations fell by about 5% by 2026 with no change in headcount, and this explains close to a third of the fall in the college wage premium.
  • Firm-level results split. Some adopters cut junior employment by about 9% while senior employment is unchanged; others, especially the biggest spenders on AI, grew and hired more entrants. Who counts as an adopter drives much of the gap, and whether substitution or scale wins is the central open question.
  • Juniors lose because their tasks are the most codifiable. AI raises novices' output most, but hiring depends on demand for the firm's output, not on output per worker. If AI does the routine work juniors used to train on, firms have less reason to hire and train them.
  • Organisation decides the direction. Management quality, the cost of reorganising and the flexibility of labour law set how deeply firms adopt. So far AI mostly saves time within existing jobs and moves knowledge down the hierarchy; new tasks appear only where employers reorganise.
  • What is still unknown. How strongly demand responds to cheaper output, how much of the fall in entry is AI rather than remote work or a slowdown that began in 2022, where the fall in entry ends as models improve, and who captures the gains.
