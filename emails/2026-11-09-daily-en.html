Gen-AI, Jobs and Growth

9 November 2026 · Today's new research on AI and work, in plain words.

AI and work, in brief

Today in three lines

  • A study of about 226,000 small online shops in China finds that those which switched on their platform's AI assistant sold about half as much again as similar shops that did not.
  • The smallest shops gained most: sales up by three fifths in the smallest quarter of shops, by a tenth in the largest.
  • It is a clean example of the question that decides what AI does to jobs: does a business use AI to do more, or to do the same with fewer hands?

New research, in plain words

Small online shops that switched on AI sold about half as much again

What it explores. Barwick and co-authors follow small sellers on a large Chinese online marketplace between October 2023 and July 2025. The marketplace offered every seller an AI assistant that writes product descriptions, produces product images and answers customer questions. About 149,000 sellers took it up; the authors compare them with about 77,000 similar sellers who did not.

Why it matters. Most studies of AI and work look at large companies in rich countries. This one looks at very small businesses, often one person, in the world's biggest online market. Their sales rose by about half, their orders by nearly as much, and the smallest shops gained the most. If that pattern holds elsewhere, AI narrows the gap between tiny firms and big ones rather than widening it.

How it fits the evidence. The review already finds that firms which adopt AI tend to grow, and that the biggest spenders hire more. This study adds the smallest firms to that picture. It cannot say what happened to jobs, because the data do not count employees; it does show that the time staff spent per order fell a little, by about one twentieth. The sellers chose to adopt, so the keenest may have joined first, and the marketplace that sells the tool also supplied the data.

How it fits together

Two forces pull in opposite directions when a firm starts using AI. One is substitution: a task that a person used to do is now done by the machine, so the firm needs fewer hours for the same output. The other is scale: the firm becomes cheaper or better, sells more, and needs more people of every kind. Today's study is a scale story: more images, more orders, more sales, and a small saving of staff time per order.

The review's other findings show the substitution side. In France, Sweden, the United States and the United Kingdom, young people entering the most AI-open occupations are hired less than before, by up to a quarter compared with other occupations. Firms are not firing; they are hiring fewer beginners. And in Danish firms with fewer than 100 employees, adopting AI went with job losses, the opposite of the Chinese shops.

So the honest summary is still the one at the top of the review: large gains on individual tasks, growth at many adopters, fewer first jobs for the young, and no visible change yet in total employment. Which force wins depends on whether cheaper output finds enough new buyers.

One idea, explained: does AI let a business do more, or the same with fewer people?

Think of a bakery that buys a much better oven. The owner can bake the same number of loaves in half the time and send a baker home, or keep everyone and bake twice as many loaves, if the town will buy them. The oven does not decide. The customers do, and so does the owner.

Economists call the first path substitution and the second scale, and the whole debate about AI and jobs turns on their balance. Studies that measure how much faster a person works with AI, often a third faster on narrow tasks, only tell us the oven is better. Studies that follow whole firms tell us what owners did with it. So far the firm studies split: some adopters cut their junior staff by about a tenth, others, especially the heaviest AI users, grew and hired more beginners. Today's Chinese shops sit on the growth side, with one caveat: a tiny shop that doubles its sales may still be a one-person shop.

For a student or a parent, the practical reading is this. The question to ask about any job is not "can AI do parts of it?" but "if AI makes this work cheaper, will people buy more of it?" Where the answer is yes, AI tends to create work. Where demand is fixed, it tends to remove it.

Where the evidence stands

Each bar counts studies on jobs, hiring or pay. Orange means the study found fewer jobs, fewer hires or lower pay; grey, no measurable effect; blue, gains. The pattern by method is the point: experiments that measure how fast people work find gains, studies of who gets hired find falls, studies of total employment find nothing yet. Today's paper would be a blue bar in the "firms that adopted" row, with the warning that it measured sales, not jobs.
