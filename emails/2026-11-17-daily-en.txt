Today's new research on AI and work, in plain words.

AI and work, in brief



Today in three lines

- A new study asks whether fast-improving AI will lift poor countries as quickly as rich ones. In most of the futures it tests, poor countries grow richer, and rich countries grow richer faster.
- In about half of those futures, the income gap between rich and poor countries widens by at least two fifths within 25 years.
- Subsidising cheap AI does little to close that gap. Helping poor countries catch up on know-how and on the human skills that pair with AI does much more.

New research, in plain words

AI may make poor countries richer, but rich countries richer still

What it explores. Daniel Björkegren, David Lagakos and Mitchell VanVuren, economists at Columbia, Boston University and Vanderbilt, build a simple model of a world with two economies, one rich and one poor, buying AI on the same global market. They run the model many times over 25 years, each time varying their assumptions about how well people and AI work together. In their scenarios, AI gets either a seventh or half again more useful every year.

Why it matters. Most forecasts of AI and economic growth focus on rich countries. Yet more than half the world's population lives in poorer ones, and the poorest countries generate only about one in a hundred visits to AI websites. The paper points to two reasons they could fall behind. They may lack the worker skills needed to put AI to good use. And fast-growing rich countries may buy up so much computing power that AI stays too expensive for everyone else.

How it fits the evidence. This is a theoretical model, not a measurement of the real world, and its outcomes span a wide range. Even so, its gloomier scenarios are hard to dismiss. Subsidising AI for poor countries gives poor value: each dollar spent brings them about eleven cents of benefit. Helping them adopt the working methods of richer economies, closing even one hundredth of that gap each year, achieves far more. One of the authors has received research funding from AI companies.

How it fits together

Inside rich countries, two forces pull in opposite directions when firms adopt AI. A company can produce more and hire more staff, or produce the same amount with fewer people. So far, the job-cutting force has shown up first, mainly through fewer entry-level jobs for young workers: up to a quarter fewer in the occupations where AI can do the most, across the United States, Sweden, the United Kingdom and France. Overall employment has not budged.

Today's paper tracks the same two forces across borders. Where AI replaces human work directly, rich countries gain the most because they can buy the most AI. Where AI needs skilled people alongside it, countries with fewer of those workers fall behind. Inside poor countries, the skilled workers whose tasks AI can take over are also the ones most likely to see their wages drop. That matches recent evidence from rich countries, where pay in the jobs most open to AI fell by about a twentieth while headcount stayed flat.

The open questions remain: how much more people will buy when AI makes things cheaper, how fast firms will reorganise, and who captures the gains. Today's paper adds another: whether those gains will cross borders at all.

One idea, explained: why the slowest step decides how fast the economy can grow

Picture a newsroom given an AI tool that writes a first draft in seconds. The newspaper still does not come out ten times faster. A reporter still has to visit the scene, call sources and check every fact. Once writing the draft costs almost nothing, those slower tasks set the limit on how many stories the paper can publish.

Philippe Aghion, Benjamin Jones and Charles Jones applied this logic to the whole economy in 2019. When new technology makes certain goods very cheap, people do not spend all their money on them. Instead, spending shifts toward things that remain hard to speed up, such as care work, construction or a haircut. Those slow sectors end up setting the pace of overall economic growth. A recent study of scientists using AI sees the same pattern in research. If AI took over every step outside the physical lab, fields that do little lab work could produce fifteen times more research. Lab-heavy fields would speed up by only three or four times, held back by work at the bench.

Today's study adds a twist: missing skills can be a slow step too, and they slow poor countries more than rich ones.

For workers and students, the practical lesson is to look for the slow steps in any job: the tasks that rely on physical presence, human judgement or trust. Those are the parts AI is least likely to make cheap.
