Gen-AI, Jobs and Growth

19 November 2026 · Today's new research on AI and work, in plain words.

AI and work, in brief

Today in three lines

  • In American federal courts, someone argues without a lawyer in about one civil case in six, up from about one in ten for two decades.
  • Yet paid legal work has not shrunk: households still spend more than before, and the biggest law firms earned about an eighth more in 2025.
  • Cheaper know-how has not emptied the profession. It shifted some work to people doing it themselves, and some to cheaper firms.

New research, in plain words

More Americans now go to court without a lawyer, and the top law firms are still doing well

What it explores. Zhang, Esposito and Yang ask what happens to a profession when AI makes expert knowledge cheap. In American legal services, they track three groups: people who might write their own court papers, companies that might hire their own lawyers, and law firms competing for the remaining work.

Why it matters. After 2022, far more people began filing their own civil cases. In civil rights disputes, which require a written complaint, the self-filed share climbed from about a quarter to nearly two in five. Prisoners, who almost always file alone anyway, saw little change, showing this is not just a broader shift in the courts. Meanwhile, companies did not bring more legal work inside: about three in ten private-sector lawyers worked in-house in both 2019 and 2024.

How it fits the evidence. The biggest law firms raised their prices. In 2025, their revenue grew by about an eighth even as their billed hours barely budged, while cheaper firms picked up more new work. Legal work is being reshuffled, not wiped out. One warning: the authors work at an AI company that builds tests for legal AI, and much of this shift between firms started before ChatGPT.

How it fits together

The review keeps coming back to two forces. AI can let a business do more, or let it do the same with fewer people. Today's study adds a third path that is easy to miss: the customer does the job alone. A tenant who drafts a complaint with a chatbot never shows up as a lost sale for a law firm, because they could not have afforded one. Household spending on lawyers stayed high, so this looks like new activity more than stolen business.

The study also echoes a broader lesson from the review: how firms organise work decides where AI takes them. In principle, companies could use AI to bring legal work in-house. So far they have not, partly because AI tools still stumble on long, complex legal tasks. In one test, the best system finished only about a quarter of them from start to finish. Instead, companies send routine matters to cheaper firms and save the hard cases for expensive ones. That explains how top firms charge more without billing more hours.

What the study cannot tell us is whether first jobs are shrinking. Across four countries, other studies in the review find up to a quarter fewer young hires in the jobs AI handles most easily. This paper does not measure junior hiring at law firms.

One idea, explained: what the growth figures cannot see

Imas and Moll (2026) make a simple point about national accounts. Growth figures add up what people pay for things, not what those things are worth to the people using them. When something gets cheaper, its weight in the figures shrinks. When it turns free, it drops out altogether. They cite estimates showing that Americans get about ten times more value from AI chatbots than they pay for them.

Come back to the bakery example. Suppose a family that used to buy a cake for every birthday learns to bake one at home with a good recipe app. The bakery sells one cake fewer, so the measured economy shrinks a little. Yet the family still gets its cake, and maybe a better one.

Today's study shows this happening in real life. Someone who writes their own court filing with a free AI tool does legal work that nobody gets paid for. In the figures, nothing happened. In practice, that person gained access to a court they could not afford before.

For a worker or a student, the takeaway is to treat headlines saying AI has not raised growth with caution. People capture some of the gains as users rather than as earners, and those benefits will not show up in pay slips or in national output for years.

Where the evidence stands

Each bar counts studies on jobs, hiring or pay. Orange marks fewer jobs, fewer hires or lower pay; grey marks no measurable effect; blue marks gains. Workplace experiments that time tasks find gains, hiring studies find drops, and studies of total employment find nothing yet. On jobs, today's paper belongs with the grey bars: lawyers did not move in-house, and law firms kept their hours. Its one clear change, more people going to court alone, shifts who does the work, something the chart does not count.
