Gen-AI, Jobs and Growth

12 November 2026 · Today's new research on AI and work, in plain words.

AI and work, in brief

Today in three lines

  • When workers can simply accept an AI's answer, beginners may never learn the job and experts may slowly lose their edge, unseen.
  • Paying people for getting things right on their own restarts the learning, and for a beginner the payments can end.
  • Over 36 years of computer science, AI research has grown fast but narrowed, and each breakthrough has worn out sooner.

New research, in plain words

Workers who can lean on AI may stop learning, unless someone pays for practice

What it explores. Researchers at the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology and IBM imagine a programmer checking code for bugs with an AI assistant. Each time, the programmer can accept the AI's verdict or check alone. Checking alone takes effort but builds skill; accepting is quick, but unused skill fades.

Why it matters. In their example, a beginner who spots four bugs in ten works next to an AI that spots about six. Accepting pays better today, so the beginner never practises. An expert only a little better than the AI stops checking too, and slowly slips. Nobody notices, because the work keeps the AI's quality.

How it fits the evidence. This is a model, not a measurement. But it gives a clear reason why firms may stop training juniors, and it prices a remedy: pay for correct answers found alone. For the beginner, payments can stop at about seven bugs in ten, when checking alone pays by itself.

AI research grew fast, but its ideas narrowed, and each breakthrough wore out sooner

What it explores. A team at the University of Chicago and Tsinghua University read the summaries of over seven million computer science papers from 1990 to 2025. They tracked which ideas appeared and faded, where researchers moved, and how sure reviewers felt.

Why it matters. Since 2017, when the design behind today's chatbots appeared, attention has bunched on a few topics after decades of stability. More than half of researchers who changed field moved into AI, and reviewers grew less sure of their verdicts. After each big breakthrough, live ideas fell back faster than before.

How it fits the evidence. Bold growth forecasts rest on research speeding up as effort pours in. This study counts papers, not output, so it cannot say growth will slow. But when everyone chases the same idea, effort buys less variety, and the next breakthrough may be harder to find.

How it fits together

The review follows two forces: AI can let a business do more and hire more, or do the same with fewer people. The clearest change so far is at the entrance. In the United States, Sweden, the United Kingdom and France, young people are hired less into the jobs most open to AI, by up to a quarter, while layoffs have not risen.

Today's first paper explains why the entrance is the weak point. Juniors used to pay for their training by doing routine work. If AI does that work, a firm has little reason to let a beginner practise on it. Experts can lose skill too, unseen until the AI fails. This is a choice: employers decide how work is split between people and machines, and whether practice is protected.

The second paper looks further ahead. Faster growth depends partly on AI research finding new ideas, and a crowded field may find them more slowly. Much is still unknown: how much demand rises when things get cheaper, where the fall in young hiring stops, and who captures the gains.

One idea, explained: why the first rung of the ladder is the first to go

Think of a bakery. The apprentice used to shape loaves all morning while the head baker handled the tricky orders. It was dull, but it was how the apprentice learned. Then the bakery buys a machine that shapes loaves. The head baker is still needed; the apprentice's morning has gone, and with it the reason to hire one.

Hiring records show the same pattern. In the United States, Seyed Mahdi Hosseini and Guy Lichtinger compared firms that started building AI into their work with similar firms that did not. A year and a half later, the adopters employed about a tenth fewer juniors, and no fewer seniors. In France, Antonin Bergeaud and colleagues found that permanent hires of people in their twenties fell by more than a quarter in the fifth of jobs most open to AI, against less than a tenth in the fifth least open. For young software developers, hires fell by about two fifths. Firms stopped hiring rather than firing.

Junior tasks go first because they are the most routine and the easiest to write down. For a student, seek work that builds judgment. For a worker, keep your skills in use. For an employer, saving on juniors today may leave no seniors tomorrow.

Where the evidence stands

Each bar counts studies on jobs, hiring or pay: orange for fewer jobs, hires or lower pay, grey for no measurable effect, blue for gains. Experiments on how fast people work mostly find gains, hiring studies find falls, and studies of total jobs find nothing yet. Neither of today's papers counts jobs, so neither gets a bar, but the training model explains how faster work and fewer hires can sit side by side.
