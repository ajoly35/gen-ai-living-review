GEN-AI, JOBS AND GROWTH
One new study on AI and work, checked against its full text.

Diener et al. (2026), "Today's Work, Tomorrow's Workforce: Incentivizing Learning in AI-Assisted Work"
Karlsruhe Institute of Technology and IBM Research, arXiv preprint, 4 November 2026. Paper. Dynamic model of workers choosing between accepting an AI's answer and solving tasks themselves; theory with illustrative numbers, no causal estimates.

What it finds. Relying on AI can trap novices at low skill and let experts lose theirs, while output quality stays at the AI's level, so the loss goes unseen. Solving by hand costs effort but builds skill; accepting saves effort and lets skill decay. Even forward-looking workers stay trapped when learning pays too late. In the authors' code-review example, a novice who finds 40% of bugs, beside an AI that finds 62%, never practises. Paying for correct unaided answers, at first 0.27 of a bug's value per task and falling with skill, carries the novice to 70%, after which payments stop.

Why it matters. It formalises the training channel in section “Why these patterns: theory”: as in Garicano and Rayo (2025), AI removes the routine work through which juniors paid for their training. It adds expert deskilling and an explicit price for practice, a choice that sits with employers (section “Organisation: who decides how AI is used”). It does not test the junior hiring falls of Hosseini and Lichtinger (2025).

Caveat. One worker, one task family and chosen parameters, not estimates. Skill grows only through unaided work; if engaged use of AI also teaches, the trap weakens, as the authors note.

Lu et al. (2026), "Stronger artificial intelligence breakthroughs hasten their own exhaustion"
University of Chicago Knowledge Lab and Tsinghua University, arXiv preprint, 19 October 2026. Paper. Descriptive analysis of 36 years of computer science papers, career moves and peer reviews, with an illustrative simulation; outside the causal evidence standard, context for AI and growth.

What it finds. Since the Transformer in 2017, computer science has grown while its attention narrowed, and each AI breakthrough was followed by faster exhaustion of its ideas. Across 7.18 million papers from 1990 to 2025, inequality of attention across keywords (a Gini coefficient) rose from 0.26 to 0.33 within eight years of 2017, after decades without change. Of 918,862 moves between subfields, 53.81% ended in AI, mostly by mid-career researchers. In 73,089 peer reviews, 2017 to 2025, reviewer confidence fell from 3.9 to 2.9 out of 5. Active ideas fell below their ceiling modestly after AlexNet, severely after the Transformer.

Why it matters. It bears on the companion review's section on automating ideas. Growth models where ideas get harder to find (Aghion, Jones and Jones 2019) treat difficulty as a property of the idea space; here it also depends on how researchers spread their attention. That weakens the research feedback loops behind explosive growth in Davidson et al., in line with the slower paths of Rungcharoenkitkul (2026).

Caveat. Nouns in abstracts stand in for ideas, and the post-2022 window is short. The paper does not link narrowing attention to output; the growth reading is ours.

MAIN IDEAS
- Large gains on tasks, none yet in the aggregates. In field experiments, generative AI raises output on narrow tasks by 14 to 34%, most for workers who were least productive before. In the Nordic registers, total employment, hours and earnings are unchanged so far.
- The adjustment runs through entry, not layoffs. Young hires in the most exposed occupations have fallen in the US, Sweden, the UK and France, by up to a quarter relative to less exposed occupations, and contract ends fell too: firms stop hiring juniors rather than dismiss workers. Hires are a small flow, so the stock of jobs moves slowly and totals look flat.
- Exposed occupations adjust through pay as well. Wages in the most exposed occupations fell by about 5% by 2026 with no change in headcount, and this explains close to a third of the fall in the college wage premium.
- Firm-level results split. Some adopters cut junior employment by about 9% while senior employment is unchanged; others, especially the biggest spenders on AI, grew and hired more entrants. Who counts as an adopter drives much of the gap, and whether substitution or scale wins is the central open question.
- Juniors lose because their tasks are the most codifiable. AI raises novices' output most, but hiring depends on demand for the firm's output, not on output per worker. If AI does the routine work juniors used to train on, firms have less reason to hire and train them.
- Organisation decides the direction. Management quality, the cost of reorganising and the flexibility of labour law set how deeply firms adopt. So far AI mostly saves time within existing jobs and moves knowledge down the hierarchy; new tasks appear only where employers reorganise.
- What is still unknown. How strongly demand responds to cheaper output, how much of the fall in entry is AI rather than remote work or a slowdown that began in 2022, where the fall in entry ends as models improve, and who captures the gains.
