Today's new research on AI and work, in plain words.

AI and work, in brief



Today in three lines

- Researchers at Anthropic interviewed people at 58 American companies about how they use AI at work. Only two had seen AI wipe out a job outright.
- Still, people at two in three companies said AI makes them hire less, and nine firms had cut outside contractors, often abroad.
- The firms gaining the most from AI redesigned jobs around it instead of just buying a tool.

New research, in plain words

Firms that use AI are not firing much, but most are hiring less

What it explores. Three economists at Anthropic, the maker of the Claude AI models, ran 66 video interviews with people at 58 of its US business customers. They then grouped nearly 300 ways these firms use AI by how much value interviewees said each use brought, and by how much the company had to change to get that value.

Why it matters. Most firms said AI helps them produce more, or make better things, rather than just cut costs. Few jobs vanished outright: automation fully removed a role at two companies, and five pushed out staff who refused to use AI, while ten hired more people because of it. Yet at two in three companies, interviewees said they now hire fewer people than they otherwise would have. Most of those missing roles are jobs the growing business never created.

How it fits the evidence. This matches what large job records already show: no wave of layoffs, just fewer new hires, especially among beginners. The interviews add two things official records miss. First, firms are cutting contractors abroad, in call centres and back offices. Second, the biggest gains go to firms that rethink who does what. Because the authors work at Anthropic and picked its own customers for variety rather than at random, treat these as well-chosen stories, not a market-wide count.

How it fits together

Our wider review keeps returning to two forces. AI can help a business do more with the same team, or do the same work with fewer people. Today's interviews show both happening at once. Most firms say AI raised their output, which is the "do more" force. At the same time, most say they hire less, which is the "fewer people" force working quietly through job ads that never go up.

That quiet hiring slowdown is the exact pattern the review finds in the United States, Sweden, Britain and France. Entry-level hiring for young people has dropped by up to a quarter in the jobs AI handles best, without any rise in layoffs. These interviews put a voice to the numbers. As one interviewee summed it up, "We may not be firing, but we're not hiring."

The interviews also back the review's point that management choices set the outcome. Three law firms used the same tool in three different ways. One automated its mailroom. One struggled to get staff to use AI well at all. The third paired junior lawyers with seniors to check and rewrite AI drafts, keeping beginners learning and everyone in a job. We still do not know how often firms pick that third path.

One idea, explained: does AI let a business do more, or the same with fewer people?

Picture a bakery that buys a much better oven. The owner can bake the same number of loaves with one baker fewer, or keep the whole team and bake twice as many, provided the town buys them. The oven does not make that call. The customers and the owner do.

Economists call the first route substitution and the second scale, and firm-level studies find both. In America, the third of firms spending the most on AI grew their overall staff by about a tenth and their beginner ranks by about an eighth (Kharazian and co-authors). Other AI-adopting American firms cut junior staff by about a tenth while keeping seniors (Hosseini and Lichtinger). Meanwhile, small Chinese online shops that switched on AI lifted sales by about half (Barwick and co-authors).

Today's interviews show both routes running side by side inside the same company. A firm can use AI to sell more while still hiring fewer people than it once would have, making output growth and slower hiring true at once.

For a student picking a career, the useful question is not "can AI do parts of this job?" It is "if AI makes this work cheaper, will people buy much more of it?" For an employer, buying the oven is the easy part. Figuring out what the bakers do next is where the real gains sit.

Where the evidence stands

Orange bars count studies finding fewer jobs, fewer hires or lower pay; grey means no measurable change; blue means gains. Workplace speed tests tend to find gains, hiring studies find drops, and studies of total employment find nothing yet. Because today's report relies on interviews rather than a statistical count, it gets no bar of its own. If it did, it would sit in the "firms that adopted" row, split in two: blue for firm output, orange for hiring.
