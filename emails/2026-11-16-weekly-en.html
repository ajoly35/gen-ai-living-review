New research, in plain words

Games designed by an AI alone beat games designed by people who used the same AI

What it explores. Economists John Conlon and Peter Schwardmann, based in Ohio and Pittsburgh, built a small online market. About 300 American volunteers improved simple games, like a falling-blocks puzzle, by instructing an AI that writes code. They then paid a few cents of real money to play each other's games. Meanwhile, the AI designed other games on its own from one short request. Later rounds brought in 180 people in creative jobs, such as game developers, along with about 550 more buyers.

Why it matters. Two answers usually come up when people ask what work will remain for humans. One is taste: the AI handles routine chores while people decide what is worth making. The other is that buyers prefer knowing a person made something. This study tests both ideas. Buyers paid 47 cents on average for an AI-designed game, compared with 37 cents for a human-designed one. Creative professionals beat the amateurs, yet still lost to the AI. Telling buyers at random who made each game changed nothing. On average, human designers expected to win and changed their minds only after reading the AI's idea.

How it fits the evidence. Most studies in our review show that AI helps people work faster. This one asks a harder question: do we still need the person? Here, the answer leans towards no. Still, designing a game took minutes, playing it took a minute, and prices were in cents. Real creative markets, built on reputations and months of work, could look quite different.

How it fits together

Our review tracks two forces. AI can help a business do more, selling more and hiring more, or let it produce the same output with fewer staff. Today's study looks at a question behind both: do people keep a role in directing the work? Optimists assume they will, since people know what other people want. In this market, humans held no such edge, at least for quick, simple products.

This matches the broader pattern in our review. So far, AI mostly saves time inside existing jobs, while new work appears only when employers reorganise. Across the United States, Sweden, the United Kingdom and France, young people are hired less into the jobs most open to AI, by up to a quarter compared with other jobs. If machines can also handle the creative steps juniors once learned on, the first rung of the career ladder narrows even further.

The human designers also enjoyed the work and felt proud of their games. If the machine takes that work over, economic growth figures will count more and cheaper games, while missing the lost satisfaction. We still do not know whether this result holds for longer, larger creative projects.

One idea, explained: three kinds of AI: replace, speed up, or spread expertise

Picture a hospital ward, where a new machine can do three quite different things. It can replace a task: a system that reads scans on its own lowers the value of the specialist who used to read them. It can speed up a task: better software lets a nurse write notes in half the time. Or it can spread expertise: a small finger clip reads blood oxygen, so a technician can do what once needed a blood test and a specialist.

Daron Acemoglu, David Autor and Simon Johnson argue that these are distinct technologies with distinct effects on workers. Replacing tasks tends to cut the share of income going to workers. The middle two may raise pay, or may not, depending on whether cheaper products bring in enough new demand. Only a fourth kind, AI that creates new tasks needing new human skills, clearly helps workers. Firms, they warn, may lean towards the first kind because it makes staff easier to replace.

Today's study shows why this matters: when the AI did the whole design, the person's role shrank. For an employer, the choice of which kind of AI to buy is partly a choice about what your staff will do. For a worker, the safer skills are those the new tools make more valuable, not those they copy.

New research, in plain words

Office workers who started using an AI assistant met their colleagues more, not less

What it explores. Researchers at Bocconi University in Milan tracked nearly 12,000 office workers at a large consumer-goods company as it rolled out Microsoft's AI assistant from September 2024 to September 2025. Logs of meetings, calls and emails showed who worked with whom. About 2,000 people became regular users, and the study compared them with similar colleagues who never used the tool.

Why it matters. Many worry that AI will turn office work into a solo job, yet people learn much of their craft from colleagues. Here the opposite happened. Over their first three months, regular users arranged about an hour and a half more of meetings, calls and emails per month than non-users. They worked with about two more people each month, and relied more on colleagues they already trusted.

How it fits the evidence. As the authors read it, other people still have to check and sign off on an AI draft, which gives colleagues new reasons to talk. Still, the study follows one company for three months, and it tracks who met whom, not what they discussed or whether the work got better.

AI could slow its own progress if it squeezes the shoppers who pay for new ideas

What it explores. An economist at the Bank for International Settlements in Basel builds a theoretical model in which AI takes over more and more tasks. Handing over each task requires building machines, which absorbs income that people would otherwise spend in shops. Yet inventors get paid out of what shoppers buy.

Why it matters. Some forecasts have growth jumping to 13 to 18 per cent a year within two decades. In this model, the sunniest scenario reaches about five and a half per cent. In the likeliest case, growth ticks up briefly, then settles almost half a point below the usual two per cent, leaving wages about a seventh lower after twenty years than in a world without AI.

How it fits the evidence. This is a theoretical model, not data from the real world, and its results rest on how fast AI improves, which nobody knows. Even so, it points to a different brake on growth: not the tasks machines cannot do, but too little consumer spending to reward the next invention.

One idea, explained: who do you ask when you are stuck? How AI changes the office pecking order

Picture a hospital ward. A junior nurse handles routine cases and takes unusual ones to a senior nurse, who calls in a doctor for the hardest problems. In 2000, Luis Garicano described firms in just this way: as ladders of knowledge, where rare, difficult problems travel up to the few people able to solve them.

Give that junior nurse a tool that answers routine questions, and fewer problems travel up the ladder, so the ward needs fewer senior staff. In 2025, Enrique Ide and Eduard Talamàs brought AI into this framework: an AI that assists workers helps beginners the most, whereas an AI that acts on its own helps top experts the most.

In the Milan study, staff with an AI assistant did not stop asking colleagues for help. They reached out to more people, and leaned harder on the ones they already trusted. The core question at work shifts from "how do I do this?" to "is this right?" For students, that means good judgment and trusted relationships still matter. For employers, it means some of the time AI saves will go into checking the output, so it pays to plan for that.

New research, in plain words

Workers who can lean on AI may stop learning, unless someone pays for practice

What it explores. Researchers at the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology and IBM picture a programmer hunting for code bugs alongside an AI assistant. On every task, the programmer can take the AI's word for it or check the code alone. Checking alone takes effort and builds skill. Accepting the AI's answer is fast, though unused skill fades.

Why it matters. In their setup, a beginner who catches four bugs in ten works beside an AI that catches about six. Taking the AI's answer pays better right away, so the beginner never practises. An expert who is only slightly better than the AI stops checking too, and slowly slips. Nobody notices, because the finished work stays at the AI's level.

How it fits the evidence. This is a theoretical model, not real-world data. Still, it offers a clear reason why firms might stop training juniors, and puts a price on a fix: pay people for right answers they find on their own. For the beginner, those extra payments can end once they catch about seven bugs in ten, when working alone pays off by itself.

AI research grew fast, but its ideas narrowed, and each breakthrough wore out sooner

What it explores. Researchers at the University of Chicago and Tsinghua University read the summaries of over seven million computer science papers published from 1990 to 2025. They tracked which ideas showed up and faded away, where researchers moved, and how confident peer reviewers sounded.

Why it matters. After decades of steady variety, research attention bunched around a few topics starting in 2017, when the design behind today's chatbots arrived. More than half of the researchers who switched fields moved into AI, and peer reviewers grew less sure of their judgments. After each big breakthrough, active ideas died out faster than they used to.

How it fits the evidence. Optimistic economic forecasts assume that pouring more effort into research speeds up discovery. This study counts academic papers rather than economic output, so it cannot prove growth will slow down. Yet when everyone crowds around the same idea, extra effort buys less variety, making the next big leap harder to find.

One idea, explained: why the first rung of the ladder is the first to go

Picture a bakery. An apprentice used to shape loaves all morning while the head baker handled tricky custom orders. Shaping dough was dull, yet that was how the apprentice learned the trade. Then the bakery buys a loaf-shaping machine. It still needs the head baker, but the morning routine is gone, and so is the reason to hire an apprentice.

Hiring data tells the exact same story. In the United States, Seyed Mahdi Hosseini and Guy Lichtinger compared companies that began weaving AI into their work with similar companies that did not. A year and a half later, the AI users employed about a tenth fewer juniors, with no drop in senior staff. In France, Antonin Bergeaud and his co-authors found that permanent hiring of people in their twenties fell by more than a quarter in the fifth of jobs where AI fits best, compared with a drop of less than a tenth in the fifth where it fits least. For young software developers, hiring fell by roughly two fifths. Companies did not fire people; they just stopped hiring.

Entry-level tasks go first because they follow clear rules and are easy to spell out. If you are a student, look for work that builds human judgment. If you are already working, keep using your core skills. And if you run a business, cutting back on juniors today may leave you with no seniors tomorrow.

New research, in plain words

In jobs AI can do, people are not losing work faster, but finding it takes longer

What it explores. Economists Alexander Cline and Barış Kaymak at the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland track Americans aged 23 to 54 from month to month in the government's jobs survey. They sort more than 900 occupations into twenty groups, from least to most open to AI. Comparing 2015 to 2019 with 2024 to early 2026, they measure how often people lost a job and how quickly those out of work found another.

Why it matters. Unemployment rose by about one extra person in every hundred in the jobs most open to AI, compared with the jobs least open to it. That rise could come from people losing their jobs or from unemployed workers struggling to get hired again. Here, it is the second. Roughly one in three unemployed people normally finds work each month, and in the jobs most open to AI, that monthly chance dropped by about a sixth more than elsewhere. Layoffs showed no clear change.

How it fits the evidence. This lines up with studies from France, Sweden and the United States: firms are hiring fewer beginners rather than laying off staff. Still, this is a first look that compares simple averages without stripping out other shifts in the economy. Because it leaves out young people hunting for their very first job, the true hit to newcomers could be larger.

How it fits together

The review tracks two forces. AI can help a business produce more and hire more, or do the same work with fewer people. Today's study shows where that second force bites first. It is not at the exit, since people in these jobs keep them at the usual rate. It is at the entrance, where people trying to get in wait longer.

One idea, explained: No wave of layoffs, yet fewer first jobs: how both can be true

Imagine a newsroom that starts using AI to draft routine stories. Nobody gets fired. Yet every year a few reporters retire or move on, and the editor stops replacing all of them. The newsroom shrinks one empty desk at a time, and the person paying the price is the graduate who would have sat there.

Job records back this up. In the United States, Lee Tucker compared people aged 22 to 24 in the fifth of industries and states most open to AI with young people elsewhere, and with older workers in the same places. Their employment dropped by about an eighth over two and a half years, mostly through fewer hires. In Sweden, Magnus Lodefalk and colleagues found that by early 2025, jobs for 22 to 25 year olds in the occupations most open to AI sat about a twentieth lower within the same employers. Meanwhile in Denmark, Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard found that workers using chatbots earn the same pay and work the same hours as before.

Both facts hold at once because hiring is a small stream feeding a large lake. Slow the stream, and the lake level barely moves for years.

For a student, landing that first job in these fields is harder, so early experience counts double. For a worker, the main risk is not a layoff, but a long job hunt after leaving. For an employer, each junior desk left empty today means a missing senior worker tomorrow.

How it fits together

The review tracks two forces: AI can let a business do more, or do the same with fewer people. Today's American paper shows where growth is clearest so far: in building AI itself. Data centres, chips and power plants add to the economy now, long before most firms change how they work. Most firms that use AI use it in one or two corners, not at the core.

Nobody yet knows when all this spending will turn into higher output per worker. Companies first have to reorganise workflows and train staff, and that can take years. The tool itself does not decide the outcome. How firms put it to work does.

One idea, explained: a job that AI could do is not a job AI does

Imagine a hospital ward gets a machine that can take blood pressure, write notes and order supplies. On paper, that machine could take over half of a nurse's tasks. On the ward, nurses might use it only to write notes because nobody has time to learn the rest.

That is the gap between what AI could do and what people use it for. Eloundou and co-authors asked human raters and an AI model which work tasks AI could speed up by half or more. They found that nearly half of US jobs could see at least half their tasks affected once the right software exists. Massenkoff and McCrory then checked which tasks people actually bring to Claude, Anthropic's AI. In computer and maths jobs, AI could in principle help with almost every task, yet only about a third show up in real-world use. Nearly a third of workers hold jobs where no real use shows up at all.

Both of today's papers group jobs by what AI could do in theory, and both warn that potential is not actual use. That is why "AI could take your job" headlines keep running ahead of the hiring numbers.

If you are a worker, the useful question is not whether AI could do your tasks, it is whether your employer is reorganising how the work gets done. If you are an employer, the productivity gains only arrive with that second step.

New research, in plain words

Freelance writing is shrinking, but AI projects are getting bigger and stranger

What it explores. Researchers at OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT, and at Upwork compare the tasks people give ChatGPT with the jobs businesses paid freelancers to do on Upwork between 2023 and mid-2026. They also track which tasks clients group into a single job post, and how much they pay.

Why it matters. Across roughly two thirds of job categories, tasks that people now hand to ChatGPT are bought less often from freelancers. Content writing stands out: posts there dropped more than twice as fast as posts overall, while video and animation gained about a third in share. Yet AI-related jobs are bigger. Compared with other posts in the same field and month, they carry fixed budgets about half as large again, and the freelancers hired log about two fifths more hours over the first three months. Hourly pay barely budges. Clients are paying for more work, not pricier hours.

How it fits the evidence. Earlier studies showed that freelance writers lost business once ChatGPT launched. This report finds the same drop, and spots a second pattern: AI-related posts often pair tasks rarely combined before, like AI-generated video and human editing. About one pair of tasks in twelve is this kind of unusual mix, compared with one in forty in other posts. Two caveats apply. Both firms benefit if AI looks good for freelancers, and the study tracks patterns without proving that AI drove them.

How it fits together

When a firm adopts AI, two forces pull in opposite directions. It can do the same output with fewer hands, or take on bigger ambitions and hire more help. Today's report catches both happening on one platform. Routine writing gigs vanish to the machine. Larger projects that build AI into a finished product, such as a video, a marketing campaign or an app, expand and demand more hours.

This matches the wider picture from our review. Across the United States, Sweden, Britain and France, hiring of young workers into the jobs most open to AI has fallen by up to a quarter. Freelance writing tells the same story on an online market: small, straightforward orders dry up. The fresh news is on the growth side, where rising demand blends skills that once belonged to separate specialists.

Nobody knows yet if this adds up to more work across the whole economy. Freelance sites are a small slice of the labour market, and the writers losing gigs are rarely the people winning video contracts. Our review points to management as the deciding factor: AI generates new work mainly when someone reshapes the job around it.

One idea, explained: saving an hour a week is not the same as a new job

Imagine a hospital ward given software that drafts patient notes, saving each nurse an hour a week. Does the ward automatically treat more patients, or start offering a new type of care? Not on its own. Without someone rethinking how the ward operates, those saved minutes just get absorbed back into the old routine.

One of the biggest workplace trials to date saw this firsthand. Dillon and co-authors gave an AI assistant inside email, meeting and writing apps to more than seven thousand office staff across 66 firms, and measured them against colleagues who worked without it. Over six months, staff with the tool cut their weekly email time by almost an hour and a half, or about an eighth. Meetings stayed just as they were, because changing a meeting takes group agreement, and the overall shape of their workweek barely shifted.

Saved time is just raw material. New work only shows up when a boss or a client redesigns the job around the tool, much like the Upwork buyers pairing AI video generation with human editing. If you are a worker, the real question is not "how much time will AI save me?" It is "what will my employer do with the hours freed up?" If you run a business, the payoff comes from rethinking workflows, not just buying software licences.

New research, in plain words

Job losses from new technology are counted before the new jobs it creates

What it explores. Mark Esposito and Liu Zhang, at the AI company micro1 and Northeastern University, ask why the job losses from a new technology appear before its gains. Existing firms that cut staff show up in payroll numbers right away. New firms take years to grow big enough to be counted.

Why it matters. In the computer era, firms founded since 1978 accounted for about two fifths of all American payroll jobs by 1998. If AI follows the same path, the next few years of data will bring bad news first and good news later.

How it fits the evidence. So far, the American industries most open to AI have not lost more jobs than others. The authors note that a slow rollout of AI would look just like this, as would no effect at all. The data cannot tell the two apart yet. Both authors work for an AI company.

Factory inspectors would give up a pay rise for AI that helps them, not for AI that replaces them

What it explores. Lara Fleck and colleagues at Maastricht University studied a large car-parts maker bringing AI into quality checks. At first, the AI flagged possible defects and left the final call to the worker. In a planned next step, it would handle the easy cases itself and leave workers only the hard ones. The researchers asked 423 inspectors, mostly in Mexico and Romania, to choose between pairs of hypothetical jobs.

Why it matters. Workers valued the assistive AI about as much as a pay rise of three per cent. By contrast, they valued AI that takes over easy tasks barely more than their old job without AI. The workers who found the assistive AI most useful disliked the second step the most, meaning a firm that rolls out AI in stages can burn the goodwill it won at the start.

How it fits the evidence. Economists debate which uses of AI make workers' skills more valuable and which wear them down. This study puts the question directly to workers. Because the answers cover hypothetical jobs inside a single firm, they tell us what workers want, not what actually happened to them.

How it fits together

Two forces pull in opposite directions when firms adopt AI. A business can do the same work with fewer people, or it can produce more and hire. The first paper explains why the job-cutting force shows up first: a firm that trims its workforce registers in this quarter's numbers, whereas a startup made viable by cheaper tools may take years to hire at scale. Much is still unknown: how quickly new firms built on AI will hire, whether cheaper output finds enough buyers, and how much of the fall in first jobs is AI rather than the slowdown that began in 2022.

One idea, explained: why two firms with the same tool get different results

Picture two hospital wards that get the same patient-monitoring system on the same day. On the first ward, the head nurse rewrites the shift schedule, trains the team, and lets nurses decide how to act on the alerts. On the second, someone switches the system on and changes nothing else. A year later, the first ward has fewer emergencies. The second has blinking alarms that everyone ignores.

Economists see the same pattern with AI. In the United Kingdom, the best-managed tenth of firms are far more likely to use AI than the worst-managed tenth, even though good management does not predict whether a firm uses robots or other machines. In France, fewer firms use AI than in Germany, roughly one in five compared with one in four. About seven in ten French workers get no encouragement, tools, or training from their employer. A study for HEC Paris's LIFT institute blames the high cost of reorganising work in France, which pushes firms to test AI in small, easily reversed ways.

What a firm builds around the tool matters more than the tool itself. For an employer, that means budgeting for training and redesigned workflows, not just software licences. For a worker, it means asking how a new tool will be brought in, and whether staff get a say.

Where the evidence stands

Each bar counts studies on jobs, hiring, or pay: orange for fewer jobs, fewer hires, or lower pay, grey for no measurable change, and blue for gains. Studies of how fast people work mostly find gains, studies of who gets hired find drops, and studies of total employment find nothing yet. Today's first paper would add to the grey in the total-employment row, and it explains why that bar could stay grey for years even as AI reshapes daily work. The second paper measures what workers prefer rather than actual jobs or pay, so it sits off the chart.
