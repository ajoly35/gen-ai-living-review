# Review update: November 2026

<p class="byline">Adrien Joly · London School of Economics · drafted by an AI assistant, approved before publication</p>

## The month in one paragraph

**Narrative nuanced.** This first update covers the 30 entries added from 17 October to 15 November 2026. Three studies pass the test to enter the review. Hyman et al. (2025) give the first evidence on how workers leaving exposed jobs move, a gap the review flags. Levy Yeyati et al. (2026) show that the wage adjustment in exposed occupations also lowers the return to schooling. Soda, Iorio and Rizzo (2026) find that Copilot users collaborate with more colleagues, which qualifies the claim that AI makes workers rely less on colleagues. The entry margin holds; descriptive monitoring from Canada, Australia and the Cleveland Fed goes to the watch list.

## Proposed changes to the review

**1. Main patterns, *Mobility* (Hyman et al. 2025).**

Now: "How displaced workers would move is not yet observed: 6.1 million US workers (4.2%) are in highly exposed occupations with low capacity to adapt (Manning and Aguirre 2026)."

Proposed: "How displaced workers move is only starting to be observed: 6.1 million US workers (4.2%) are in highly exposed occupations with low capacity to adapt (Manning and Aguirre 2026). Across 1.9 million US publicly funded training spells from 2012 to 2024, Hyman et al. (2025) find that trainees from highly exposed occupations earned about &#36;900 a quarter more than matched job seekers who received only job search help before 2020, and &#36;2,900 more by 2022 to 2024 (2010 dollars), mostly by moving into less exposed occupations; the comparison matches on observed characteristics only, and 2022 to 2024 was a tight labour market."

**2. Exposure designs: wages (Levy Yeyati et al. 2026).** Added after the sentence ending "28 to 32% of the observed fall (Azar et al. 2026a)."

Proposed: "Inside exposed occupations, the price of schooling fell too: comparing the top and bottom quarters of occupations by exposure in US Current Population Survey earnings from 2018 to June 2026, Levy Yeyati et al. (2026) find that after ChatGPT's release the return to a year of schooling fell by 0.55 log points from 7.5, about 7%, while the return to a year of experience rose by 0.26 log points at three years and fell by 0.12 at twenty-five, with no differential trend before 2022; the schooling estimate is sensitive to how top-coded wages, frequent in exposed occupations, are treated."

**3. Who holds knowledge? (Soda, Iorio and Rizzo 2026).**

Now: "AI moves knowledge down the hierarchy, so workers rely less on superiors and colleagues."

Proposed: "AI moves knowledge down the hierarchy, so workers rely less on superiors, though not necessarily on colleagues."

Added after the Hoffmann et al. (2025) sentences: "Assistants can also add contact: in one multinational consumer-products firm, the 1,992 knowledge workers who adopted Microsoft's Copilot between September 2024 and September 2025 initiated 1.4 more hours of collaboration a month in their first three months of use than 9,876 colleagues who never adopted, mostly in meetings and calls, and worked with about 2.2 more distinct colleagues (Soda, Iorio and Rizzo 2026), although adoption was partly chosen and the effect averaged over all months is smaller and not significant."

**4. Main ideas, *Organisation decides the direction*.**

Now: "So far AI mostly saves time within existing jobs and moves knowledge down the hierarchy; new tasks appear only where employers reorganise."

Proposed: "So far AI mostly saves time within existing jobs and moves knowledge down the hierarchy, though in one firm users spent part of that time working with more colleagues; new tasks appear only where employers reorganise."

## To watch

- Barwick et al. (2026): Chinese small sellers that adopted their platform's AI assistant raised sales by about half against matched non-adopters. Full text to be read next month; it is the strongest candidate for the scale side of the firm-level split.
- Unemployment, not only hiring: Cline and Kaymak (2026) find job finding fell about 6 points more in the most exposed US occupations, which would qualify the review's reading of Imas and Schaal (2026) that unemployment shows no effect. A design with a pre-trend test would settle it.
- Countries without an exposure gap: in Canada and Australia, young hiring fell about as much in low-exposure jobs. If a causal design confirms this, "the US, Sweden, the UK and France" would need a counterweight in the entry idea.
- Entry before the labour market: Finnish applications to exposed degrees fell 14% per standard deviation of exposure by 2026; worth tracking as a channel for the review's eligibility term.
- Inside AI firms: juniors fell from 27% to 9% of technical hires at Anthropic while total hiring grew; a case study, but the clearest within-firm picture yet.
