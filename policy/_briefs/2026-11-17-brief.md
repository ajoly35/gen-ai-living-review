## Facts

News peg: on 17 November 2026 the French news channel BFMTV titled its morning economics editorial
"IA : les premiers emplois menacés" (AI: first jobs under threat). Only the title is known; do not describe its content.

Table (official unemployment rates, seasonally adjusted; read 17 November 2026):
- France (INSEE, third quarter of 2026): 8.2%, against 7.7% a year earlier. Under 25: 21.0%, against 19.3%.
- United Kingdom (ONS, July to September 2026, published 17 November 2026): 4.6%, against 5.0% a year earlier. Aged 16 to 24: 15.1%, against 15.3%.
- United States (BLS, October 2026): 4.1%. Aged 16 to 24: 9.3%. No figure exists for October 2025, so no yearly comparison.
- US, share of employed adults using generative AI for work (Bick, Blandin and Deming tracker): 40.5% in August 2026, against 37.4% a year earlier.
- The table covers the whole labour market and cannot show what part, if any, is due to AI. Countries measure differently, so each is compared with itself.

Evidence (Banque de France, Bloc-notes Éco 463, 8 October 2026, Palladino, Bergeaud, Bertheau, Bunel and Degos; French
administrative records of hires, January 2019 to March 2026):
- Permanent-contract (CDI) hires of people aged 21 to 29 in the fifth of occupations where AI can do the most tasks were about 28% below their November 2022 level in March 2026, against 8% below in the fifth where it can do the least.
- The gap is smaller for people in their thirties and forties and almost absent from age 50.
- Total employment in firms that use AI heavily has not clearly diverged from other firms: the change comes through fewer hires, not through layoffs.
- Authors' caution: higher interest rates, the post-Covid correction in technology firms and remote work hit the same jobs over the same period, so the fall cannot be put down to AI alone.

France, measure: plan "Emploi futur", presented 7 May 2026 by labour minister Jean-Pierre Farandou. 15 measures. Target: employment rate of people aged 15 to 24 at 40% by 2030, from 34.3% at the end of 2025. Includes "InserScore", an indicator for every initial vocational course giving the share of leavers in work and their median pay. According to the ministries, the measures need neither a new law nor extra budget. The plan was not designed around AI.

United Kingdom, measure: package announced 6 June 2026 by the technology and work departments. An Early Careers Jobs Alliance of government, employers, unions and young people, backed by £20 million, to map how AI changes entry-level jobs and help firms redesign them. A pilot of free AI bootcamps in Lancashire and Greater Manchester for young people at risk of leaving school after GCSEs, with a guaranteed paid apprenticeship for those who finish. From early 2027 in the North East, at least 6 months of hands-on AI job training for people aged 18 to 24 who are not in work, education or training. Wider frame: the Youth Guarantee, £820 million, including 55,000 guaranteed jobs for young people in long-term unemployment. Of the three measures described in this issue, it is the only one aimed at AI and entry-level jobs (say exactly that; do not claim no other such measure exists anywhere).

United States, proposals (none voted as of 23 October 2026, per CBS News): Senators Mark Warner and Josh Hawley's AI-Related Job Impacts Clarity Act would make large companies and federal agencies report AI-related layoffs to the Department of Labor, which would publish them. Senator Ron Wyden's Stability and Opportunity for Workers Act would extend unemployment benefits to 52 weeks if the unemployment rate reaches 8.5% (the usual maximum is 26 weeks) and fund retraining for workers displaced by AI. What the evidence says: both bills are built around layoffs, while the change measured so far is in hiring; a young person who was never hired is neither laid off nor entitled to benefits.

Option 1, guidance. Problem: pupils and students choose courses on past outcomes, while junior hiring is changing fastest in some of the occupations those courses lead to. Option: publish, next to course outcomes such as France's planned InserScore, how hiring of people under 30 is moving in the occupations each course leads to; the hiring records used by the Banque de France already exist by occupation and age. Not known: measures of what AI can do in an occupation are not forecasts, and part of the fall may reverse with the economic cycle; steering a whole cohort away from a field can create a shortage later.

Option 2, income. Problem: unemployment insurance is built on past work, so it does not reach people who have not yet had a first job, and that is where the change is. Options on the table: guaranteed paid placements, as in the UK's 55,000 guaranteed jobs; longer benefits tied to the unemployment rate, as in the Wyden bill, which reaches people who have already worked. Not known: whether the fall in junior hiring is lasting or temporary, which decides whether temporary placements are enough; no study yet measures what these schemes do for young people in occupations where AI is changing hiring.
