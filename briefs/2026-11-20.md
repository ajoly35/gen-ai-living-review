# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">20 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- Office workers who imagined doing a task with AI said they would then ask a colleague for help less, most of all when brainstorming.
- When the AI chat was visible to the team, most of that pull away from colleagues disappeared.
- A second study finds that cheaper computing would lift national output by a few per cent, while leaving some workers stuck in the wrong industries.

## New research, in plain words

### Workers who lean on AI say they would ask their colleagues for help less

**What it explores.** Chow and co-authors asked 248 American office workers who use AI every week to read ten short work scenarios. In some, the task had been done with an AI assistant; in others, without one. After each, people said how much they would want a colleague's feedback.

**Why it matters.** Asking a colleague is how newcomers learn and how teams build trust. After AI help, that wish fell by about a quarter of a point on a five-point scale, and by about two fifths for brainstorming. Handing the whole task to the AI, rather than just getting help, made no extra difference.

**How it fits the evidence.** Last month, one firm's real records showed the opposite: workers who started using an AI assistant met more colleagues. This study asks about imagined situations, and its volunteers already trust AI. Its most useful finding: when the AI conversation was visible to the team, people kept turning to colleagues.

### If computing gets a third more productive, the world gets a little richer and some workers get stuck

**What it explores.** Dueñas and Mandel build a computer model of 49 economies and 163 industries, based on what industries bought from each other in 2022. They ask what happens if the firms that supply computing, software and telecoms become about a third more productive over ten years.

**Why it matters.** Output rises everywhere, by about three per cent in the European Union and nearly six in China. But farming and food shrink in some countries, such as Mexico and India, and workers are pulled into digital industries, mostly out of services. If moving is hard, the equivalent of about one German worker in a hundred is left where employers would rather shed staff.

**How it fits the evidence.** This is a scenario, not a measurement, and it covers all digital industries, not AI alone. Its lesson is that the national total hides the people who must change jobs.

## How it fits together

The review keeps coming back to two forces: AI can let a business do more, or do the same with fewer people. Today's model is a story of doing more. Cheaper computing makes the economy a little larger, but where moving between industries is slow, the pressure lingers.

The first study speaks to who holds knowledge at work. Juniors have always learned by asking more experienced colleagues. If AI answers instead, the newcomer gets the answer but misses the conversation. That sits beside one of the review's main findings: fewer first jobs for the young in the work AI does best, by up to a quarter in four countries.

Both studies also say that organisation decides the direction. A team that uses AI in the open keeps people talking; a country where changing industry is easy spreads the gains. Still unknown: whether the drop in asking for help shows up in real offices, and whether it slows how fast newcomers learn.

## One idea, explained: handing a decision to a machine

Think of a hospital ward. A junior doctor unsure about a dose can ask the senior on duty, or now an AI assistant that never sighs at a question at three in the morning.

The first experiments on handing decisions to AI show two things. In Herdağdelen, Joren and Tamkin (2026), experienced analysts working with an AI agent took the option it quietly favoured about six times in seven, and few gave it credit afterwards. Among startup founders, Colaiacovo and Koning (2026) found that how much work firms handed to AI depended less on what founders believed AI could do than on whether they had redesigned their workflows around it.

Today's study adds a third point: helping or doing the whole task made no difference to how much workers then wanted their colleagues. Privacy did.

For a worker, the lesson is to keep asking people, especially early in a career: the conversation teaches more than the answer. For an employer, how AI is set up, in the open or behind closed doors, shapes the team as much as the tool does.

## Where the evidence stands

![Studies on jobs, hiring or pay, by research design. Orange: fewer jobs, hires or lower pay. Grey: no measurable effect. Blue: gains.](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/assets/vitals/direction-by-design-en.png)

Each bar counts studies on jobs, hiring or pay. Orange means fewer jobs, hires or lower pay; grey, no measurable effect; blue, gains. Experiments that time people at work find gains, studies of who gets hired find falls, and studies of total employment find nothing yet. Neither of today's papers would add a bar: the first measures intentions, the second is a scenario, though its gains in total with costs for some echo the chart's mix of blue and orange.
