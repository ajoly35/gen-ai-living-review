# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">16 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- A new paper argues that job losses from AI show up in the statistics years before the jobs that new firms create, so early figures look worse than the truth.
- About 400 factory inspectors value AI that helps them decide as much as a small pay rise, and AI that takes over their easy work hardly at all.
- Both point the same way: what AI does to work depends on how firms bring it in, and on how long we wait before judging.

## New research, in plain words

### Job losses from new technology are counted before the new jobs it creates

**What it explores.** Mark Esposito and Liu Zhang, at the AI company micro1 and Northeastern University, ask why the job cost of a new technology appears before its benefits. Existing firms that need fewer staff show up in payroll figures at once. New firms take years to grow big enough to be counted.

**Why it matters.** In the computer era, firms founded since 1978 held about two fifths of all American payroll jobs by 1998. If AI follows the same path, the next few years of data should bring bad news first and good news later.

**How it fits the evidence.** So far, American industries most open to AI have not lost more jobs than others. The authors say that is what a slow spread of AI would produce, but also what no effect at all would look like: the data cannot yet tell them apart. Both authors work for an AI company.

### Factory inspectors would give up a pay rise for AI that helps them, not for AI that replaces them

**What it explores.** Lara Fleck and colleagues at Maastricht University studied a large manufacturer in the car industry that was bringing AI into quality checks. First the AI flagged possible mistakes and left the decision to the worker. Next it would take over the easy cases, leaving workers only the hard ones. The researchers asked 423 inspectors, mostly in Mexico and Romania, to choose between pairs of imaginary jobs.

**Why it matters.** Workers valued the helping AI about as much as a pay rise of three per cent. They valued AI that takes over the easy work barely more than the old job. Those who found the helping AI most useful liked the second step least, so a firm rolling out AI in steps may lose goodwill it first won.

**How it fits the evidence.** Economists argue about which kinds of AI raise the value of workers' skills and which wear them away. This study asks workers directly. The answers concern imagined jobs in one firm, so they show what workers want, not what happened to them.

## How it fits together

Two forces pull in opposite directions when firms start using AI. A business can do the same work with fewer people, or it can do more and hire. The first paper explains why the first force is seen sooner: a firm that trims staff appears in the figures this quarter, while a new firm made possible by a cheaper tool may take years to hire. That fits the review's main finding. Across the United States, Sweden, the United Kingdom and France, young people are hired less into the jobs most open to AI, by up to a quarter, while total employment has not moved.

The second paper fits another finding: organisation decides the direction. The same tool can be set up to help a worker or to take work away, and workers clearly prefer the first.

Much is still unknown: how quickly new firms built on AI will hire, whether cheaper output finds enough buyers, and how much of the fall in first jobs is AI rather than the slowdown that began in 2022.

## One idea, explained: why two firms with the same tool get different results

Picture two hospital wards that receive the same new monitoring system on the same day. On the first, the head nurse rewrites the rota, trains everyone and lets nurses decide how to use the alerts. On the second, the system is switched on and nothing else changes. A year later, the first ward has fewer emergencies. The second has alarms that nobody reads.

Economists find the same with AI. In the United Kingdom, the best-managed tenth of firms are clearly more likely to use AI than the worst-managed tenth, while good management does not predict the use of robots or other machines. In France, fewer firms use AI than in Germany, about one in five against one in four. About seven in ten French workers get no encouragement, tools or training from their employer. A study for HEC Paris's LIFT institute blames the cost of reorganising, which is high in France, so firms use AI in small, easily reversed ways.

The tool matters less than what a firm does around it. For an employer, that means budgeting for training and for changing the work, not only for the licence. For a worker, it means asking how the tool will be introduced, and whether staff have a say.

## Where the evidence stands

![Studies on jobs, hiring or pay, by research design. Orange: fewer jobs, hires or lower pay. Grey: no measurable effect. Blue: gains.](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/assets/vitals/direction-by-design-en.png)

Each bar counts studies on jobs, hiring or pay: orange for fewer jobs, hires or lower pay, grey for no measurable effect, blue for gains. Studies of how fast people work find gains, studies of who gets hired find falls, and studies of total employment find nothing yet. The first of today's papers would add to the grey in the total-employment row, and explains why that row may stay grey for years even while AI changes work. The second measures what workers want, not jobs or pay, so it sits off the chart.
