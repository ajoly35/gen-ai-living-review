# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">18 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- When an AI tool started checking code on volunteer software projects, the people in charge of those projects ended up with more work, not less: about a tenth more changes to look at.
- The AI made each check quicker, but it also drew in more submissions, many of them sent by automated accounts, and those were the slowest to get accepted.
- The lesson goes beyond software: when AI makes work cheaper to produce, the human who has to check it can become the bottleneck.

## New research, in plain words

### An AI reviewer gave the people who run software projects more work, not less

**What it explores.** Much of the world's software is written in the open. Anyone can propose a change to a project, and a few people in charge, called maintainers, decide what gets in. In February 2025 GitHub, the main home of these projects, offered an AI reviewer that comments on each proposed change. Researchers at Tilburg University followed about 4,000 popular projects, comparing roughly 700 maintainers who switched the reviewer on with about 1,900 who had not, month by month, from March 2024 to February 2026.

**Why it matters.** Once maintainers switched the tool on, they handled about a tenth more proposed changes than those who had not yet done so, and wrote about a seventh more comments on each. People sent a burst of extra changes in the first month, then fewer than before, and smaller ones. Changes sent by automated accounts kept rising, and took longer to be accepted. So the tool sped up each check but added to the pile.

**How it fits the evidence.** The review finds that AI so far mostly saves time inside existing jobs, and that new kinds of work appear only when organisations change how they work. This study shows a third outcome: a tool meant to save time created more work for the people whose judgement is final. Two warnings: maintainers chose to switch the tool on, and these are volunteer projects, not paid teams.

## How it fits together

Two forces pull against each other when AI arrives at work. AI can let people do the same work with fewer hands, or it can make the work cheaper so that more of it gets done. Today's study is a small, clear case of the second force. Reviewing became cheaper, so more changes came in, and the humans in charge ended up busier.

It also says something about who decides how AI is used. The maintainers handed the first look at each change to a machine but kept the final say. That is how most firms use AI today: as a helper, with a person signing off. The review finds that such choices, not the tool itself, decide whether AI removes work or creates it. Here, keeping a human at the end made that human's time the scarce thing.

Most of the evidence on jobs still points elsewhere: fewer first jobs for young people in the occupations most open to AI, by up to a quarter in the United States, Sweden, the United Kingdom and France, and in some places lower pay, with total employment not yet moving. Today's paper does not count jobs or pay. It adds a warning: the work of checking can grow as fast as the work of producing.

## One idea, explained: how economists tell an effect from a coincidence

Suppose a hospital ward gets a new heart monitor and, a month later, fewer patients there have complications. Was it the monitor? Or winter ending, or a new head nurse? To know, you need a second ward that did not get the monitor, so you can see what would have happened anyway.

That is the basic trick behind the most trusted studies of AI and work. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond studied about 5,000 customer support staff at a large software company. The company gave them an AI chat assistant, but not all at once: different teams got it on different dates. That let the researchers compare people who already had the tool with people who did not yet have it, in the same weeks. Calls resolved per hour rose by about a seventh on average, and by about a third for the fifth of staff who had been slowest.

Today's software study uses the same idea, comparing maintainers who switched the AI on with those who had not yet done so. Its weak spot is the one to watch for everywhere: people chose to adopt, and those who chose may have been different from the start.

For a student, a worker or an employer, the practical rule is this. When someone says "AI did this", ask: compared with whom?

## Where the evidence stands

![Studies on jobs, hiring or pay, by research design. Orange: fewer jobs, hires or lower pay. Grey: no measurable effect. Blue: gains.](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/assets/vitals/direction-by-design-en.png)

Each bar counts studies on jobs, hiring or pay: orange for fewer jobs, hires or lower pay, grey for no measurable effect, blue for gains. Studies of how fast people work find gains, studies of who gets hired find falls, and studies of total employment find nothing yet. Today's paper measures workload, not jobs or pay, so it adds no bar. It helps explain why the fast gains in the first rows do not turn straight into fewer jobs: time saved on one task can be eaten up by more work arriving at the next.
