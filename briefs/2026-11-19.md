# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">19 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- In American federal courts, about one civil case in six now has someone arguing without a lawyer, up from about one in ten for two decades.
- Yet paid legal work has not shrunk: households still spend more than before, and the biggest law firms earned about an eighth more in 2025.
- Cheaper know-how did not empty the profession. It moved some work to people doing it themselves, and some to cheaper firms.

## New research, in plain words

### More Americans now go to court without a lawyer, and the top law firms are still doing well

**What it explores.** Zhang, Esposito and Yang ask what happens to a profession when AI makes expert knowledge cheap. They look at three groups in American legal services: people who might write their own court papers, companies that might hire their own lawyers, and law firms competing for the work that remains.

**Why it matters.** The study finds that people filing their own civil cases rose sharply after 2022. Cases about civil rights, which need a written complaint, went from about a quarter self-filed to nearly two in five. Prisoners, who almost always file alone anyway, saw little change, which helps separate the new trend from general shifts in the courts. Meanwhile companies did not move more lawyers in-house: about three in ten private-sector lawyers worked inside companies in both 2019 and 2024.

**How it fits the evidence.** The biggest firms raised their prices. Their revenue grew by about an eighth in 2025 while the hours they billed barely moved, and cheaper firms gained more new work. That is a story of work being reshuffled, not destroyed. One warning: the authors work for an AI company that builds tests of legal AI, and much of the reshuffling between firms began before ChatGPT.

## How it fits together

The review keeps coming back to two forces. AI can let a business do more, or let it do the same with fewer people. Today's study adds a third path that is easy to miss: the customer does the job alone. A tenant who drafts a complaint with a chatbot never shows up as a lost sale for a law firm, because they could not have afforded one. Household spending on lawyers stayed high, so this looks like new activity more than stolen business.

The study also fits the review's point that organisation decides the direction. Companies could in principle use AI to bring legal work in-house. So far they have not, partly because AI tools still fail at long, complex legal tasks: in one test the best system fully completed only about a quarter of them. Instead, companies send routine work to cheaper firms and keep the hard cases with the expensive ones. That is how the top firms can charge more while doing no more hours.

What the study does not show is fewer first jobs. Elsewhere the review finds up to a quarter fewer young hires in the jobs AI can do most, in four countries. Junior hiring at law firms is not measured here.

## One idea, explained: what the growth figures cannot see

Imas and Moll (2026) make a simple point about national accounts. The growth figures add up what people pay for things. They do not add up what things are worth to the people who use them. When something gets cheaper, its weight in the figures shrinks. When it becomes free, it drops out altogether. They cite estimates that Americans get about ten times more value from AI chatbots than they pay for them.

Think of a bakery again. A family that used to buy a cake for every birthday learns to bake one at home with a good recipe app. The bakery sells one cake fewer, and the economy, as measured, shrinks a little. Yet the family still has its cake, and perhaps a better one.

Today's study is a real example. A person who writes their own court filing with a free AI tool does legal work that no one is paid for. In the figures, it looks like nothing happened. In their life, they got access to a court they could not afford before.

For a worker or a student, the lesson is to be careful with headlines that say AI has not raised growth. Some of the gains go to people as users, not as earners, and they will not show up in pay slips or in national output for years.

## Where the evidence stands

![Studies on jobs, hiring or pay, by research design. Orange: fewer jobs, hires or lower pay. Grey: no measurable effect. Blue: gains.](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/assets/vitals/direction-by-design-en.png)

Each bar counts studies on jobs, hiring or pay. Orange means fewer jobs, fewer hires or lower pay; grey, no measurable effect; blue, gains. Experiments that time people at work find gains, studies of who gets hired find falls, and studies of total employment find nothing yet. Today's paper would sit with the grey bars for jobs: lawyers did not move in-house and law firms kept their hours. Its one clear change, more people going to court alone, is a change in who does the work, which the chart does not count.
