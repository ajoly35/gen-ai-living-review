# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">21 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- Two Federal Reserve economists find that Americans in the jobs AI can do most of now take longer to find work, while layoffs in those jobs barely changed.
- Unemployment in that group rose by close to one point since ChatGPT arrived, more than twice as much as in jobs AI barely touches.
- Their old pay edge has faded too: the job stays, but it is harder to get and pays less than it would have.

## New research, in plain words

### People in jobs AI can do find new work more slowly, though few are being laid off

**What it explores.** Hie Joo Ahn and Nicholas Carollo, at the Federal Reserve Board, use the monthly survey of American households from 2012 to May 2026. They sort workers by how much of their job AI could do, and by whether employers in their industry are advertising for AI skills. Then they follow who loses a job, who finds one and who changes employer.

**Why it matters.** Unemployment can rise in two ways: more people lose their jobs, or those looking take longer to find one. Here it is the second. For workers in jobs AI can do most of, in industries that are taking AI up, unemployment rose by close to one point between the end of 2022 and May 2026. For workers in jobs AI barely touches, in industries that are not using it, it rose by less than half a point. Job losses moved little. People changed employer less often but changed tasks within their job more often: firms seem to reshuffle work rather than let people go.

**How it fits the evidence.** It is the clearest national picture yet of what smaller studies found: AI shows up as fewer hires, not more firings. The unemployment level the country settles at in normal times may have risen by about a fifth of a point, roughly one worker in five hundred, an uncertain estimate. Rising interest rates and a cooling of tech hiring came at the same time, and early 2026 shows some recovery.

## How it fits together

The review keeps two forces in view. AI can let a business do more and hire more, or do the same work with fewer people. Today's study sits on the second side, in a quiet form. Nobody is shown the door; when a job seeker knocks, the opening is less likely to be there. That is the pattern the review finds for young people in the United States, Sweden, the United Kingdom and France, where first jobs in the work AI does best fell by up to a quarter compared with other jobs. Today's paper says it is not only the young: anyone looking for work in those jobs waits longer.

It also adds a point about pay. Before ChatGPT, workers in jobs AI can now do saw their pay grow faster than others; that advantage has faded. This matches the review's finding that pay in the jobs most open to AI fell by about a twentieth by 2026 compared with other jobs, with no change in the number employed. Jobs adjust first through who gets hired and what they are paid, long before they disappear.

Organisation still decides the direction: employers are redrawing who does what within jobs. Whether that ends in new tasks or fewer posts is still unknown, as is how much of the slowdown is AI rather than higher interest rates.

## One idea, explained: when the job stays but the pay falls

Picture a newsroom. An AI tool now writes first drafts of routine stories. Nobody is fired; the same sub-editors come in each morning. But when one leaves, the paper hires a replacement on a lower salary, because the job asks less and fewer papers compete for such workers.

That is the idea behind the work of Azar and co-authors (2026). They compare American jobs that AI can do much of with jobs it can do little of. By 2026, pay in the first group had fallen by about a twentieth relative to the second. The number of people employed did not change. An employer survey gives the same answer. And because graduates more often work in the jobs AI can do, this pay squeeze explains close to a third of the recent fall in the extra pay a degree brings.

Why would pay fall in a job AI makes more productive? Either employers need fewer such workers, or more people can now do it. Since hiring into these jobs fell, weaker demand is the likelier reason.

For a worker, the lesson is that a job that survives AI may still pay less. For a student, it is worth asking not only "will this job exist?" but "will it still pay a premium?"

## Where the evidence stands

![Studies on jobs, hiring or pay, by research design. Orange: fewer jobs, hires or lower pay. Grey: no measurable effect. Blue: gains.](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/assets/vitals/direction-by-design-en.png)

Each bar counts studies on jobs, hiring or pay: orange for fewer jobs, hires or lower pay, grey for no measurable effect, blue for gains. Experiments that time people at work find gains, studies of who gets hired find falls, studies of total employment find nothing yet. Today's paper would add orange to the hiring row. It also explains the grey in the total row: slower hiring adds up to only a small rise in national unemployment.
