# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">22 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- A study of two and a half million research papers finds that, three years after ChatGPT, science looks much as it would have anyway, apart from some favourite words.
- That matters for growth: the boldest forecasts assume AI will speed up research, and whole fields show little sign of it yet.
- The same pattern holds for jobs: large gains on single tasks, little change yet in the big totals.

## New research, in plain words

### Three years of chatbots changed scientists' words, not yet the way science is done

**What it explores.** Ron Bekkerman and three colleagues at the New Jersey Institute of Technology read two and a half million research papers from 2015 to 2025, in computer science, mathematics, physics, health and life sciences. For each feature, from the words used to the size of the author team, they ask: did its path change after late 2022, when chatbots spread, or did it carry on?

**Why it matters.** Writing changed. Out of about fourteen thousand common words, roughly eight hundred became much more frequent after 2022, words like "underscore" that chatbots favour, and about two hundred plain words such as "good" became rarer. "Delve" shot up, peaked in 2024 and fell back as models changed and readers learned to spot it. But the deeper features did not break. The variety of vocabulary, the share of technical terms, the range of topics and the size of research teams all kept to the paths they were on before 2022. The exceptions: more papers in computer science and physics, and bigger computer science teams in Asia.

**How it fits the evidence.** Some fear AI is flooding science with careless writing; others hope it will speed up discovery. Across whole fields, this study finds neither yet. It compares each field only with its own past, so it cannot prove what AI did, and counting words says little about the quality of ideas.

## How it fits together

The review's first finding is large gains on single tasks, no visible change yet in the totals. In experiments, people with AI get between a seventh and a third more done on some tasks. In Denmark, total employment, hours and earnings have not moved. Today's paper finds the same gap in science. Earlier this month, a study of how researchers use one AI assistant suggested today's tools could at most double a team's output. Across whole fields, the change so far is small.

This matters beyond science. Forecasts of very fast growth rest on AI speeding up the making of new ideas. If research has not yet changed gear, the economy-wide boost is still ahead, if it comes at all. Slow steps such as lab work and checking may set the pace.

AI can let a team do more, or do the same with fewer people. In science it looks, so far, like a little more: more papers in some fields, not fewer researchers. In jobs, AI shows up mainly as fewer first jobs for the young, down by up to a quarter in the most AI-open work in the United States, Sweden, the United Kingdom and France. How much of that is AI, and how much everything else that changed after 2020, is still unknown.

## One idea, explained: was it AI, or working from home?

Picture a hospital ward that gets a new computer system in 2023. A year later, fewer junior nurses are hired. Was it the computer? Perhaps. But the ward also changed its shifts after the pandemic. Two things changed at once, and the hiring figures alone cannot say which mattered.

Economists face exactly this. ChatGPT arrived in late 2022, just after the big shift to working from home. Deming (2026) argues that remote work prepared the ground for AI by turning office work into digital records. Emanuel and co-authors (2026) show that when a large firm closed its offices, young engineers lost most of the feedback they got from sitting near colleagues. The jobs most open to AI are also those most often done from home, where juniors learn less.

Untangling them is hard. Comparing occupations, much of what looks like an AI effect vanishes once remote work is counted. But inside firms that adopted AI, junior jobs fell just as much in work that cannot be done from home. Today's paper makes the same point for science: a jump in medical papers in 2023 was most likely a rebound from a pandemic dip, not a chatbot effect.

For an employer or a student, the lesson is to ask what else changed at the same time before blaming, or crediting, AI.

## Where the evidence stands

![Studies on jobs, hiring or pay, by research design. Orange: fewer jobs, hires or lower pay. Grey: no measurable effect. Blue: gains.](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/assets/vitals/direction-by-design-en.png)

Each bar counts studies on jobs, hiring or pay: orange for fewer jobs, hires or lower pay, grey for no measurable effect, blue for gains. Experiments that time people at work find gains, studies of hiring find falls, studies of total employment find nothing yet. Today's paper is about science, not jobs, so it has no bar. Its message is closest to the grey: across a whole field, as across a whole economy, little has moved so far.
