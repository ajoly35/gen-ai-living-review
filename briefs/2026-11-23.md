# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">23 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- American companies most open to AI agents, programs that carry out whole chains of tasks on their own, posted about one in twenty fewer job adverts after agents got better, mostly for junior roles.
- A second study follows the money behind AI's data centres: companies may build about half again more than society needs, much of it on borrowed money.
- Both point the same way: hopes for AI are moving hiring plans and investment faster than they are moving the economy.

## New research, in plain words

### Firms most open to AI agents cut junior job adverts once agents improved

**What it explores.** Nicola Borri and three colleagues study AI agents, programs that plan, run software and act, not just answer questions. They spot the listed American companies whose share price moves most when agent use jumps, then count their job adverts, 35 million of them, from 2022 to mid-2026.

**Why it matters.** In late September 2025, Anthropic released a much stronger coding agent, and agent use took off. In the next six months, the firms most open to agents posted about one in twenty fewer adverts than similar firms less open to them. Before the release, the two groups had moved together. The fall was in junior roles; senior adverts and the pay offered held.

**How it fits the evidence.** It matches what studies of chatbots found: firms hire fewer beginners rather than lay people off. But adverts are not hires, and share prices move for many reasons, so this is an early warning.

[Full entry](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/latest/#borri-et-al-2026-the-rise-of-agents-and-the-labor-market) · [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27210)

### The race to build AI data centres may overshoot, and debt spreads the risk

**What it explores.** Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh and Zijin Su, at Columbia, map who owes what to whom in the AI building boom: chipmaker NVIDIA, the cloud giants, two AI labs and data-centre landlords, tied by loans, leases, contracts and shares in each other.

**Why it matters.** In their model, firms build about half again more computing power than is best for society, for over six trillion dollars, because each counts its own new sales but not those it takes from rivals. About three fifths is paid with debt. If AI disappoints badly, outside investors lose about seventeen trillion dollars, and lenders about two fifths of what they put in.

**How it fits the evidence.** Fast-growth forecasts assume this building happens; the bigger and more indebted the boom, the harder a disappointment lands. Its numbers rest on assumptions, so they show the size of the risk, not a prediction.

## How it fits together

AI can let a business do more, or do the same with fewer people. So far the second force shows up mainly at the door. Young hires in the jobs most open to AI have fallen by up to a quarter in the United States, Sweden, the United Kingdom and France, while totals stay flat. Today's agent study finds the same: fewer junior adverts, no firing.

Pay can adjust too: elsewhere, pay in the jobs most open to AI fell by about a twentieth. Here it held, so firms seem to post fewer junior jobs rather than cheaper ones.

Doing more with AI needs huge data centres, and firms racing each other may build too many on borrowed money. What is still unknown is how much demand cheaper AI will create, and so whether this building pays off in output and jobs, or in losses.

## One idea, explained: why people expect more job loss than the data show

Picture a bakery that buys a new oven. The brochure says it can bake almost anything by itself. A year later, the oven has sped up a few steps. Nobody has been sacked, but the bakery took on one apprentice fewer.

That is roughly where AI and jobs stand. Sacher and colleagues at the Anthropic Institute (2026) asked about eleven thousand American adults what AI will do by 2030. Workers thought AI could do over a quarter of their working hours by then. Those who expected AI to do most of their own work also expected far more unemployment: about one worker in eight, against seven or eight in a hundred for everyone else. The data so far show no rise in unemployment linked to AI, only fewer openings for beginners.

Expectations matter even when they run ahead of the facts: they move share prices, hiring plans and trillions of dollars of building.

For a student, the risk lies at the first rung: getting in may be harder than staying in. For a worker, fear is not a forecast. For an employer, cutting junior hiring on a hope can leave you short of experienced staff later.

## Where the evidence stands

![Studies on jobs, hiring or pay, by research design. Orange: fewer jobs, hires or lower pay. Grey: no measurable effect. Blue: gains.](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/assets/vitals/direction-by-design-en.png)

Each bar counts studies on jobs, hiring or pay: orange for fewer jobs, hires or lower pay, grey for no measurable effect, blue for gains. Experiments that time people at work find gains, studies of hiring find falls, studies of total employment find nothing yet. Today's study of AI agents would join the orange part of the hiring bar: fewer junior adverts. The study of data-centre finance is about money, not jobs, so it has no bar.
