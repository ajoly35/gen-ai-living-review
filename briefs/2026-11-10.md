# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">10 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- In a test market for simple computer games, games designed by an AI on its own sold for about a quarter more than games designed by people working with the same AI.
- Telling buyers that a game was made by a person did not make them pay more, and the people who designed games did not see it coming.
- It tests two popular hopes: that human taste stays in charge, and that buyers pay extra for the human touch.

## New research, in plain words

### Games designed by an AI alone beat games designed by people who used the same AI

**What it explores.** John Conlon and Peter Schwardmann, economists in Ohio and Pittsburgh, built a small market online. About 300 American volunteers improved simple games, like a falling-blocks puzzle, by giving instructions to an AI that writes code. Then they paid a few cents of real money to play each other's games. Other games were designed by the AI alone, from one short request. Later rounds added 180 people in creative jobs, such as game developers, and about 550 more buyers.

**Why it matters.** Two answers often come up when people ask what will be left for humans. One is taste: the AI does the routine work, people decide what is worth making. The other is that buyers like knowing a person made something. This study tests both. Buyers paid 47 cents on average for an AI-designed game against 37 cents for a human-designed one. The professionals beat the amateurs but not the AI. Buyers were told who made the game at random, and that made no difference. On average, designers expected to win, and changed their minds only once they read the AI's idea.

**How it fits the evidence.** Most studies in the review find that AI helps people work faster. This one asks a harder question: is the person still needed? Here, the answer leans towards no. But design took minutes, games were played for a minute and prices were in cents. Real creative markets, with reputations and months of work, may differ.

## How it fits together

The review follows two forces. AI can let a business do more, selling more and hiring more, or it can let it do the same with fewer people. Today's study is about a question that sits behind both: whether people keep a role in directing the work. Hopeful accounts assume they will, because people know what other people want. In this market they had no such edge, at least in quick, simple products.

It fits the review's pattern. So far AI mostly saves time inside existing jobs, and new work appears only where employers reorganise. In the United States, Sweden, the United Kingdom and France, young people are hired less into the jobs most open to AI, by up to a quarter compared with other jobs. If the creative steps juniors once learned on can also be done by the machine, the first rung of the ladder gets thinner still.

The designers also enjoyed the work and were proud of their games. If the machine takes it over, the growth figures will show more and cheaper games, but not the lost satisfaction. What is still unknown is whether this holds for longer, larger creative projects.

## One idea, explained: three kinds of AI: replace, speed up, or spread expertise

Think of a hospital ward. A new machine can do three quite different things there. It can replace a task: a system that reads scans on its own makes the expertise of the person who used to read them less valuable. It can speed up a task: a nurse with better software writes up notes in half the time. Or it can spread expertise: a small clip on the finger reads the oxygen in the blood, so a technician can do what once needed a blood test and a specialist.

Daron Acemoglu, David Autor and Simon Johnson argue that these are different technologies with different effects on workers. Replacing tasks tends to lower the share of income that goes to workers. The middle two can raise pay, or not, depending on whether cheaper products bring in enough new demand. Only a fourth kind, AI that creates new tasks needing new human skills, is clearly good for workers. They add that firms may lean towards the first kind, because it makes workers easier to replace.

Today's study shows why this matters: when the AI did the whole design, the person's role shrank. For an employer, the choice of which kind of AI to buy is partly a choice about what your staff will do. For a worker, the safer skills are those the new tools make more valuable, not those they copy.

## Where the evidence stands

![Studies on jobs, hiring or pay, by research design. Orange: fewer jobs, hires or lower pay. Grey: no measurable effect. Blue: gains.](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/assets/vitals/direction-by-design-en.png)

Each bar counts studies. Orange means fewer jobs, fewer hires or lower pay; grey, no measurable effect; blue, gains. Experiments that measure how fast people work with AI mostly find gains, studies of who gets hired find falls, and studies of total employment find nothing yet. Today's paper does not measure jobs, so it gets no bar. If it did, it would sit with the experiments but lean orange: the AI alone beat the people using it, which points to less need for human designers, not more output per person.
