# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">28 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- A new study asks whether AI doing scientists' work could make the economy grow faster and faster. Its answer: it depends on two things nobody yet knows.
- On cautious assumptions, growth rises from under three per cent a year to a little under four, around 2090. On bold ones, it explodes in the 2060s, then hits an energy limit.
- Either way, the tasks people still do hold everything back for decades, the same brake the review sees in today's workplaces.

## New research, in plain words

### AI could speed up growth a little or enormously, and the deciding factors are still unknown

**What it explores.** Three economists, Wim Naudé, Tiago Neves Sequeira and Derick Almeida, model an economy where AI gradually takes over the tasks of research. New ideas raise output, output pays for more computers, and more computers produce more ideas. They ask when this loop would speed up growth, and what would stop it, running the model from 2023 under four sets of assumptions.

**Why it matters.** Forecasts about AI and growth run from barely noticeable to explosive. This study shows the same economy can end up at either extreme, depending on how well AI can stand in for human researchers and how fast good ideas get harder to find. On cautious US estimates, growth peaks at a little under four per cent a year around 2090. If extra research mostly repeats work already done, growth never speeds up. If ideas stay as easy to find as they have been for computer chips, growth reaches extreme rates in the 2060s, until the energy to run the machines runs short.

**How it fits the evidence.** It agrees with the most careful earlier work: tasks that still need people act as a brake. For decades, adding computers barely helps, because people remain the slow step. Until almost nothing in research needs a human, gains come from handing tasks to machines one by one. This is a model, not a measurement, and the two deciding factors have never been measured for AI.

## How it fits together

The review follows two forces inside firms: AI can let a business do more with the same people, or the same with fewer. Today's study asks the question for the whole economy, over a century rather than a few years. Its message is close to what the firm studies show. What AI could do in principle is not what it does in practice, and the parts of the work that still need people set the pace.

That brake is visible in today's labour market. In the United States, Sweden, the United Kingdom and France, young people are hired less into the jobs most open to AI, by up to a quarter, yet total employment has not moved. Firms that reorganise around AI gain most; firms that just buy the tool gain little. Tasks move to machines one at a time, and the totals change slowly.

The unknowns are the same at both levels: how far AI can replace people in the hardest tasks, and how much more people buy when things get cheaper. Today's study adds a third for the long run: how much energy the machines will need.

## One idea, explained: why the slowest step decides how fast the economy can grow

Think of a hospital ward that gets an AI able to read every scan and draft every report in seconds. Patients do not leave ten times faster. A nurse still has to turn them, a doctor still has to talk them through the choice. Once the paperwork is nearly free, those slow steps set the pace of the whole ward.

Philippe Aghion, Benjamin Jones and Charles Jones made this point for the whole economy in 2019. When a technology makes some things very cheap, spending shifts to what is still hard to do, like care, building work or teaching. Those slow parts then decide overall growth. A recent study of scientists' work found the same thing in research: fields with little lab work could gain far more from AI than fields where the lab bench is the slow step.

Today's study shows how long such a brake can last. In its cautious version, AI adds almost nothing to research for decades, then its contribution jumps within a generation once the last human steps are covered.

For a worker or an employer, look for the slow steps in a job, the parts that need presence, judgement or trust. That is where people stay needed longest, and where better organisation pays off most.

## Where the evidence stands

![Studies on jobs, hiring or pay, by research design. Orange: fewer jobs, hires or lower pay. Grey: no measurable effect. Blue: gains.](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/assets/vitals/direction-by-design-en.png)

Orange bars count studies finding fewer jobs, fewer hires or lower pay; grey, no measurable effect; blue, gains. Experiments on how fast people work find gains, studies of who gets hired find falls, and studies of total employment find nothing yet. Today's paper models growth rather than counting jobs, so it has no bar. It does help explain the grey row: if the tasks people still do hold everything back, big gains on single tasks can sit alongside little change in the totals for a long time.
