# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">27 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- Researchers at Anthropic interviewed people at 58 American companies about how they use AI at work. Only two had seen AI wipe out a job outright.
- Yet at two in three companies, people said they now hire less because of AI, and nine had cut outside contractors, often abroad.
- The firms that got the most out of AI were those that redesigned jobs around it, not those that simply bought a tool.

## New research, in plain words

### Firms that use AI are not firing much, but most are hiring less

**What it explores.** Three economists at Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI models, held 66 video interviews with people at 58 of its business customers in the United States. They then sorted nearly 300 ways these firms use AI by how much value people said each brought, and by how much the firm had to change to get it.

**Why it matters.** Most firms said AI helps them make more, or make better things, rather than simply cutting costs. Few jobs were lost outright: automation fully removed a position at two companies, and five pushed out staff who refused to use AI, while ten hired more people because of AI. But at two in three companies, people said they now hire fewer people than they otherwise would. Most of these are jobs never created as the business grew.

**How it fits the evidence.** This matches what large job records already show: no wave of layoffs, but fewer new hires, especially of beginners. It adds two things those records cannot see. One is cuts to contractors abroad, in call centres and back offices. The other is that the biggest gains came where firms changed who does what. The firms are all Anthropic customers, picked for variety rather than at random, and the authors work for Anthropic. These are well-chosen stories, not a measurement.

## How it fits together

The review keeps coming back to two forces. AI can let a business do more with the same people, or do the same with fewer people. Today's interviews show both at once. Most firms say AI raised what they produce, which is the "do more" force. Yet most also say they hire less, which is the "fewer people" force working quietly through jobs that are never posted.

That quiet route is the pattern the review finds in the United States, Sweden, Britain and France: fewer first jobs for the young in the work AI does best, by up to a quarter, with no rise in layoffs. The interviews put a face on those numbers. As one person put it, "We may not be firing, but we're not hiring."

The interviews also back the review's point that organisation decides the direction. Three law firms used the same tool in three ways. One automated its mailroom. One struggled to get anyone to use AI well. The third paired junior lawyers with seniors to check and rework AI drafts, so beginners kept learning and nobody lost a job. What is still unknown is how often firms choose that third path.

## One idea, explained: does AI let a business do more, or the same with fewer people?

Think of a bakery that buys a much better oven. The owner can bake the same number of loaves with one baker fewer, or keep everyone and bake twice as many, if the town will buy them. The oven does not decide. The customers and the owner do.

Economists call the first path substitution and the second scale, and studies of whole firms find both. In America, the third of firms that spent most on AI grew their staff by about a tenth and their beginners by about an eighth (Kharazian and co-authors). Other American firms that adopted AI cut their junior staff by about a tenth while keeping their seniors (Hosseini and Lichtinger). Small Chinese online shops that switched on AI sold about half as much again (Barwick and co-authors).

Today's interviews show the two paths running side by side. A firm can sell more with AI and still hire fewer people than it would have, so growth and fewer jobs can both be true.

For a student, the useful question about a career is not "can AI do parts of this job?" but "if AI makes this work cheaper, will people buy more of it?" For an employer, the oven is the easy part. Deciding what the bakers do next is where the gains are.

## Where the evidence stands

![Studies on jobs, hiring or pay, by research design. Orange: fewer jobs, hires or lower pay. Grey: no measurable effect. Blue: gains.](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/assets/vitals/direction-by-design-en.png)

Orange bars count studies that found fewer jobs, fewer hires or lower pay; grey, no measurable effect; blue, gains. Experiments that measure how fast people work find gains, studies of who gets hired find falls, and studies of total employment find nothing yet. Today's report is interviews, not a count, so it does not get a bar of its own. If it did, it would sit in the "firms that adopted" row and be split: blue for what firms produce, orange for how many people they hire.
