# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">25 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- Big firms that gave programmers an AI coding assistant hired more than a quarter fewer technical staff a year later, and about two fifths fewer juniors. Layoffs did not rise.
- Two new models say AI lifts growth more slowly than many hope, unless we save far more to build the machines.
- The first jobs go first, and the big gains take longer than headlines promise.

## New research, in plain words

### Companies that switched on an AI coding assistant hired fewer young programmers, but did not fire more

**What it explores.** Lindsey Raymond and Eleanor Dillon follow about 5,400 large companies using GitHub, a service for managing software, which launched an AI coding assistant in 2023. Big firms buy tools at contract meetings fixed long in advance, so some got it earlier only because their meeting came sooner. The authors compare them with firms still waiting.

**Why it matters.** Firms that took up the assistant hired more than a quarter fewer technical staff a year later, and about two fifths fewer junior software engineers. Experienced hiring held up, and departures did not rise. Beginners gain most from AI on a single task, yet they are hired less.

**How it fits the evidence.** It is the cleanest test yet of a pattern seen in several countries: firms hire fewer beginners rather than fire anyone. One author works at Microsoft, which owns GitHub and supplied the data.

[Full entry](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/latest/#raymond-and-dillon-2026-ai-assisted-programming-and-labor-demand) · [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31083)

### Machines drove almost all past growth, but the jobs people still do slow AI down

**What it explores.** Charles Jones and Christopher Tonetti measure how much of seventy years of US growth came from machines taking over tasks, then look ahead.

**Why it matters.** Had machines been stuck with the tasks they did in 1950, about nine tenths of the gain from automation would have vanished. Ahead, growth speeds up, but slowly: if AI follows past patterns, income per person in 2050 is only about a seventh higher than it would otherwise be.

**How it fits the evidence.** It sides with the cautious forecasts: the economy moves only as fast as its slowest links, the tasks people still do. One author also works at Anthropic, an AI company.

[Full entry](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/latest/#jones-and-tonetti-2026-past-automation-and-future-ai-how-weak-links-tame-the-growth-explosion) · [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w36050)

### Whether AI brings a boom may depend on who saves for the machines

**What it explores.** Jesús Fernández-Villaverde imagines machines able to do every task by 2045, and asks how much households would save to build them.

**Why it matters.** With the same machines, the economy grows by more than a tenth a year if households save as much as suits them, but only about a fiftieth if they keep saving under a quarter of income, as today. People who live only on wages and own nothing could lose up to two thirds of what they can afford.

**How it fits the evidence.** It answers the paper above: slow growth may come from too little saving. Both are thought experiments, not forecasts.

[Full entry](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/latest/#fernndez-villaverde-2026-saving-for-the-machines) · [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35964)

## How it fits together

A firm that adopts AI can do more and hire more, or do the same with fewer people. The coding study shows the second force at work: not cutting staff, but taking in fewer beginners. That matches the review's finding for young people in the US, Sweden, the UK and France: up to a quarter fewer first jobs in the occupations most open to AI, compared with other occupations.

The growth papers ask whether the whole economy will speed up. Jones and Tonetti say yes, but over decades; Fernández-Villaverde says saving matters as much. Both warn that wages could fall far behind output once machines do most tasks, so who owns the machines matters. Still unknown: whether cheaper software brings enough new customers for firms to hire again.

## One idea, explained: is a degree worth less in the age of AI?

Think of a hospital ward. A new nurse brings textbook knowledge; an experienced one, a feel for when a patient is turning worse, learned over hundreds of night shifts. Put the textbook in every phone on the ward, and book learning is no longer rare. Judgement still is.

Two recent studies find this in pay. Azar and co-authors find that pay in the jobs most open to AI fell by about a twentieth by 2026, compared with jobs much less open to it, while the number of people doing them held steady. Because graduates hold more of those jobs, this explains about three tenths of the recent fall in the extra pay a US degree brings. Levy Yeyati and co-authors find that, in the jobs most open to AI, the extra pay from each year of schooling shrank by about a fourteenth after ChatGPT, while the extra pay from the first years of experience rose.

So a degree still pays, but more of its value now comes from what you learn by doing. For a student: collect practice as well as courses. For an employer, today's coding study is a warning: if you stop hiring beginners, who becomes the experienced staff you prefer?

## Where the evidence stands

![Studies on jobs, hiring or pay, by research design. Orange: fewer jobs, hires or lower pay. Grey: no measurable effect. Blue: gains.](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/assets/vitals/direction-by-design-en.png)

Orange means fewer jobs, fewer hires or lower pay; grey, no measurable effect; blue, gains. Experiments on how fast people work find gains, studies of who gets hired find falls, studies of total employment find nothing yet. The coding study adds to the orange in the "firms that adopted" row, though total staff held steady. The growth papers model the future, so they have no bar.
