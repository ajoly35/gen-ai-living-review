# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">26 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- German bosses would use AI instead of hiring a beginner for routine work, much less for work that needs judgement.
- In Europe, AI growth could widen budget deficits: wages are taxed at nearly twice the rate of profits.
- Where AI is bought abroad, a country can work faster and still earn less.

## New research, in plain words

### Managers would swap a beginner for AI on routine work, much less on work that needs judgement

**What it explores.** Vincent Rost and Fabian Stephany asked the bosses of about 1,400 German firms, in summer 2026: if you needed extra hands for good, would you use AI rather than hire a beginner? Half were told the work was routine, like keeping data up to date. The other half were told it needed judgement.

**Why it matters.** Bosses facing routine work were clearly more ready to pick AI. Looking two years ahead, nearly a third expect AI to cut their hiring of beginners, and a quarter expect fewer experienced hires too.

**How it fits the evidence.** Studies in several countries find firms hiring fewer young people, not firing staff. This one suggests why: beginners get the routine tasks, the easiest to hand to a machine. These are stated plans, not actual hires.

[Full entry](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/latest/#rost-and-stephany-2026-will-ai-replace-juniors-how-task-complexity-reduces-automation-risk) · [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34099)

### Growth from AI could leave European governments shorter of money, not richer

**What it explores.** Spencer Bastani and three Uppsala colleagues model a European economy where machines take over more tasks, and ask what happens to the budgets that pay for schools and hospitals.

**Why it matters.** In their main scenario the economy grows by about an eighth, yet the budget gap widens by nearly one euro in every hundred produced. More income goes to machines and their owners, and profits are taxed much less than wages.

**How it fits the evidence.** Many hope AI will pay for ageing societies. This works only if public services grow more slowly than the economy, or taxes change. It is a model, not a measurement.

[Full entry](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/latest/#bastani-et-al-2026-ai-automation-and-fiscal-pressure) · [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34234)

### When AI is imported, a country can work faster and end up poorer

**What it explores.** Eduardo Levy Yeyati looks at countries like Brazil and Mexico whose firms buy AI "agents", programs that carry out whole tasks, from foreign companies. An accounting firm serves more clients per hour, but part of what customers pay goes abroad.

**Why it matters.** Once wages cannot fall further, handing local service work to foreign AI lowers the country's income. If the seller keeps more than about a sixth of the savings, output falls too. Exporters are safer: they can sell more abroad to pay.

**How it fits the evidence.** It adds a question to who gains from AI: not only workers or owners, but which country. It is also a model.

## How it fits together

AI's first mark on jobs is fewer first jobs. In the United States, Sweden, the United Kingdom and France, young people entering the jobs most open to AI are hired less, by up to a quarter compared with other jobs. Firms are not firing. Today's German study gives the bosses' side: it is not youth that makes beginners easy to replace, but the routine work they are given.

A firm can use AI to do more, and hire, or to do the same with fewer people. German managers mostly picture the second path, most of all for beginners. Organisation decides the direction: give beginners work that needs judgement and the case for hiring them holds.

The growth papers turn to an open question: who captures the gains. If income moves from wages to profits, European states collect less. If it goes to foreign AI sellers, countries like Brazil can lose income as work speeds up.

## One idea, explained: students are already voting with their feet

Picture a town that hears new machines can shape bread. Before any baker loses a job, fewer teenagers train as bakers. They react to what they expect.

That is what Domnisoru and Heikkinen (2026) find in Finland. Between 2022 and 2026, students putting computing first fell by more than a third, while applications to other subjects rose by about a sixth. Degrees more open to AI lost applicants faster, and only after 2023. Yet the jobs of recent graduates from those degrees had moved only a little.

Students may be ahead of the job market, wisely or not: computing courses in Finland still draw more applicants than places. Today's German study hints at what matters more than the subject: routine starter tasks are the ones at risk.

For a student, the lesson is to seek courses and jobs that train judgement, not only routine skills. For an employer, fewer applicants today means fewer experienced staff in ten years.

## Where the evidence stands

![Studies on jobs, hiring or pay, by research design. Orange: fewer jobs, hires or lower pay. Grey: no measurable effect. Blue: gains.](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/assets/vitals/direction-by-design-en.png)

Orange means fewer jobs, fewer hires or lower pay; grey, no measurable effect; blue, gains. Experiments on how fast people work find gains, studies of who gets hired find falls, studies of total employment find nothing yet. None of today's papers adds a bar: the German study records what managers say they would do, though it leans orange, and the growth papers model the future.
