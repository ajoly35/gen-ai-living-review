# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">24 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- AlphaFold2, the AI tool that predicts the shape of proteins, did not send biologists towards unknown proteins. Where its guesses were surest, they studied known proteins more.
- First experiments on unexplored proteins fell by close to half in the places where the tool was most confident. Follow-up work on known proteins rose by about a fifth.
- It is the same pattern the review finds in jobs: AI takes over the first step, and people pile into the work that builds on it.

## New research, in plain words

### Where an AI tool guessed protein shapes well, scientists stopped exploring and dug deeper instead

**What it explores.** Proteins do most of the work in our cells, and their shape decides what they do. Finding one shape used to take a lab more than a year and could cost up to a few hundred thousand dollars. In 2021 AlphaFold2 began predicting the shape of almost every known protein, but it is surer for some than for others. Myra Mohnen and Joshua Gans follow about half a million proteins from 2017 to 2025. They compare what labs did on proteins with sure predictions and on proteins with shaky ones, before and after the tool came out.

**Why it matters.** Many hopes for faster economic growth rest on AI speeding up science. The question is also which direction. Where predictions were surest, the chance that a protein far from anything already known got its first real experiment fell by close to half. Follow-up experiments on proteins already known rose by about a fifth, and studies of proteins working with partners by about a quarter. Total experiments rose by about an eighth. So the tool made science busier, but closer to home.

**How it fits the evidence.** Two other recent papers point the same way. One finds that big AI breakthroughs draw researchers into the same crowded areas, which wears them out faster. Another finds that, three years after chatbots, the shape of science has barely changed. The drop in first experiments faded after 2023, so this may be a pause rather than a lasting turn.

## How it fits together

The review's main finding on jobs is that AI hits the first rung. Young people entering the occupations most open to AI are hired less, by up to a quarter in the United States, Sweden, the United Kingdom and France, while older workers keep their jobs. Today's paper finds the same shape in a laboratory. The tool does the first step, finding a shape, so fewer labs do it themselves.

That matters for growth for the same reason it matters for careers. Junior work is where people learn. First experiments are where science learns what it did not know. If AI makes both less worth doing, the short run looks good: more output, more follow-up papers, more sales. The long run depends on who still explores. Today's paper also notes that future AI tools learn from the experiments scientists choose to run, so fewer first experiments today mean thinner training data tomorrow.

What is still unknown is whether this lasts. Firms may hire juniors again once they reorganise, as some big AI users already do. Labs may return to the frontier, as the fading drop after 2023 hints. How people organise, not the tool, decides the direction.

## One idea, explained: can a worker displaced by AI retrain into something better?

Picture a hospital ward. A medical secretary types up letters from doctors' notes. Then a voice tool writes the letters itself. The secretary could look for the same job at another hospital, where the same tool is coming. Or the secretary could train as a nursing assistant, a job that needs hands and presence, and that AI cannot do.

Hyman, Lahey, Ni and Pilossoph (2025) study exactly this choice in the United States. They follow job seekers who took publicly funded training courses, and compare them with similar job seekers who only got help looking for work. Those who came from jobs most open to AI earned about &#36;900 more every three months after training. By 2022 to 2024 that gap had grown to about &#36;2,900, mostly because trainees moved into jobs less open to AI. In about two in five occupations, workers even earned more after moving into work more open to AI, not less.

For a worker, the lesson is that training pays most when it leads to a different kind of work, not the same work elsewhere. For an employer, it shows that people from jobs AI changes can be hired and retrained. For a student, it is a reason to pick skills that combine with AI, or that it cannot touch.
