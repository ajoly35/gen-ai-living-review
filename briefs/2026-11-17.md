# AI and work, in brief
{:.no_toc}

<p class="byline">17 November 2026 · Adrien Joly, London School of Economics · <a href="{{ '/briefs/' | relative_url }}">All briefs</a></p>

## Today in three lines

- A new study asks whether fast-improving AI will make poor countries richer as quickly as rich ones. In most of the futures it tries, poor countries gain and rich countries gain more.
- In about half of those futures, the income gap between rich and poor countries grows by at least two fifths within 25 years.
- Handing out cheap AI does little to close the gap. Helping poor countries catch up on know-how and on the skills that work alongside AI does much more.

## New research, in plain words

### AI may make poor countries richer, but rich countries richer still

**What it explores.** Daniel Björkegren, David Lagakos and Mitchell VanVuren, economists at Columbia, Boston University and Vanderbilt, build a simple model of a world with two economies, one rich and one poor, that buy AI from the same global market. They run it many times, each with a different guess about how well AI and people work together, over 25 years. AI becomes either a seventh or half again more useful every year.

**Why it matters.** Most forecasts of what AI will do to growth are made for rich countries. Yet more than half of the world's people live in poorer ones, and the poorest countries account for only about one in a hundred visits to AI websites. The study points to two reasons they could fall behind. They may lack the skills that make AI useful. And rich countries, growing fast, may keep buying so much AI that its price stays out of reach.

**How it fits the evidence.** This is a model, not a measurement, and the range of outcomes is very wide. Still, its worrying cases cannot be ruled out. Paying for poor countries' AI use works badly: each dollar spent is worth about eleven cents to them. Helping them adopt the methods of richer economies, closing even one hundredth of the gap each year, works far better. One of the authors has received research funding from AI companies.

## How it fits together

Inside rich countries, two forces pull against each other when a firm starts using AI. It can do more and hire more, or do the same with fewer people. So far the second force shows up first, in fewer first jobs for the young: up to a quarter fewer in the occupations most open to AI, in the United States, Sweden, the United Kingdom and France. Total employment has not moved.

Today's study looks at the same forces between countries. When AI can do the work that people did, rich countries gain most, because they can afford the most AI. When AI needs people beside it, countries short of those people gain less. The study also finds that, inside poor countries, the skilled workers whose tasks AI can do are the ones most likely to see their pay fall. That echoes what the review finds in rich countries, where pay in the jobs most open to AI fell by about a twentieth with no change in headcount.

The open questions remain: how much more people will buy when AI makes things cheaper, how fast firms will reorganise, and who captures the gains. Today's paper adds another: whether those gains will cross borders at all.

## One idea, explained: why the slowest step decides how fast the economy can grow

Think of a newsroom that gets an AI able to write a first draft in seconds. Yet the paper does not come out ten times faster. Someone still has to go to the scene, call the sources and check every fact. Once drafting is nearly free, those slow steps decide how many stories the newsroom can publish.

Philippe Aghion, Benjamin Jones and Charles Jones made this point for the whole economy in 2019. When a technology makes some goods very cheap, people do not spend ever more on them. Spending shifts to the goods and services that are still hard to make, like care, building work or a haircut. Those slow parts then set the pace of overall growth. A recent study of how scientists use AI finds the same thing in research. If AI took over every step that does not need hands in a lab, fields with little lab work could do fifteen times more research. Lab fields would gain only three or four times, because the lab bench is the slow step.

Today's study adds a twist: missing skills can be a slow step too, and they slow poor countries more than rich ones.

For a worker or a student, the lesson is to look for the slow steps in a job: the parts that need presence, judgement or trust. Those are the parts AI is least likely to make cheap.

## Where the evidence stands

![Studies on jobs, hiring or pay, by research design. Orange: fewer jobs, hires or lower pay. Grey: no measurable effect. Blue: gains.](https://ajoly35.github.io/gen-ai-living-review/assets/vitals/direction-by-design-en.png)

Orange means fewer jobs, fewer hires or lower pay; grey, no measurable effect; blue, gains. Experiments that measure how fast people work find gains, studies of who gets hired find falls, and studies of total employment find nothing yet. Today's paper is a model of the future, not a count of what has happened, so it adds no bar. Its forecast for skilled workers whose tasks AI can do, lower pay, matches the orange in the rows on pay.
