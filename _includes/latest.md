New papers on AI and labour markets and AI and growth, newest first, each placed against the review below. Every figure is checked against the paper itself.

### Naudé, Neves Sequeira and Almeida (2026), "From Automation to Acceleration: Transitional Regimes in AI-Driven Growth"
<!-- kind: paper -->

IZA Discussion Paper 19110, November 2026. [Paper](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/19110/from-automation-to-acceleration-transitional-regimes-in-ai-driven-growth). Semi-endogenous growth model of AI in research, calibrated to US automation shares, four scenarios; theory, not a causal design.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether AI that takes over research tasks can make the economy grow ever faster, and when that would start and stop.
Why it matters: Forecasts range from no change to explosive growth. The same economy can end up at either, depending on two unknowns.
How it fits the evidence: It agrees that tasks people still do hold growth back, and adds that energy limits, not automation, end any boom.

**What it finds.** Growth depends less on how much research is automated than on how easily AI replaces researchers and how fast ideas get harder to find. Naudé, Neves Sequeira and Almeida (2026) fit an automation frontier to US task shares (0.28 in 1950, 0.75 in 2023, from Jones and Tonetti 2026), set the AI–researcher substitution elasticity at 0.2, and simulate from 2023. With the idea parameters of Bloom et al. (2020), output growth peaks at 3.6% in 2090. With heavy duplication of research effort, it never exceeds 2.7%. With Moore's Law parameters, it peaks at 96% in 2066; an energy ceiling then cuts it to about 25% by 2123.

**Where it fits.** Companion review, automating ideas and long-run growth: like Jones and Tonetti (2026), it turns the Aghion, Jones and Jones (2019) bottleneck into a dated path. The Davidson et al. explosion appears only with Moore's Law parameters, and energy caps it. Early speed-ups come from handing tasks to machines, not from AI capital, which qualifies the accumulation channel in Trammell and Korinek.

**Caveat.** The frontier is fitted to automation in private business, not research, and timing hinges on parameters nobody has estimated for AI, above all how easily AI substitutes for researchers.

### Wilmers, Massenkoff and McCrory (2026), "Working with AI"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Anthropic Economics research report, 23 November 2026. [Report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/working-with-ai). Interviews at 58 US enterprise customers of Anthropic, coded by an AI model. Descriptive qualitative study, outside the review's causal evidence standard; reported as context.

**In brief.**
What it explores: How 58 American companies using Anthropic's AI are changing jobs, hiring and team roles around it.
Why it matters: Firms report hiring fewer people rather than firing, and say the biggest gains came where they redesigned jobs.
How it fits the evidence: It matches the pattern of fewer new hires without layoffs, and the view that organisation decides what AI does to work.

**What it finds.** Firms say AI mostly raises output rather than cutting costs, and their most valued uses required the most reorganisation. Wilmers, Massenkoff and McCrory (2026) held 66 video interviews in 58 US companies, and coded nearly 300 AI applications by reported value and depth of organisational change. Automation fully eliminated a job at only two companies; five pushed out workers who would not adopt AI; 10 hired more because of AI. People at two-thirds of the companies reported hiring less because of AI, mostly by forgoing hires needed to grow. Nine cut contractors, often offshore. Six startups said they would need four times as many staff without AI.

**Where it fits.** Section [“Organisation: who decides how AI is used”](#organisation-who-decides-how-ai-is-used): reported value rises with the depth of reorganisation, which extends Dillon et al. (2025), where saved time did not become new tasks without redesign. Fewer hires without layoffs match Hosseini and Lichtinger (2025) and the entry margin in [Main patterns](#main-patterns). Cuts to offshore contractors are a margin that national registers cannot see.

**Caveat.** Interviewees are Anthropic customers chosen for variety, not at random, and the authors work at Anthropic. Value is reported by interviewees and coded by an AI model, not measured.

### Rost and Stephany (2026), "Will AI Replace Juniors? How Task Complexity Reduces Automation Risk"
<!-- kind: paper -->

ZEW Mannheim and Oxford Internet Institute, arXiv preprint, 25 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34099). Pre-registered survey experiment with managers of 1,435 German firms; outcomes are stated choices, not realised hiring.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether managers would use AI instead of hiring a beginner, and whether that depends on how routine the beginner's work is.
Why it matters: If only routine starter work is at risk, beginners who take on tasks needing judgement keep a way in.
How it fits the evidence: It supports the view that juniors lose because their tasks are easiest to write down as rules, not because they are junior.

**What it finds.** Managers are less willing to replace a junior with AI when the work needs judgement. In the ZEW business survey, fielded June to August 2026, managers of German information-economy and manufacturing firms were randomly shown a lasting need for either standardised tasks or tasks needing judgement and context. Complex tasks lower willingness to use AI instead of hiring by 0.54 points on a five-point scale, from a mean of 3.21, and raise the importance of human review by 0.15 points, from 4.32. Asked before the scenario, 31% expect AI to cut their entry-level hiring over two years and 25% their experienced hiring; 5.5% and 10.1% expect increases.

**Where it fits.** Section [“Why these patterns: theory”](#why-these-patterns-theory): it tests the codifiability mechanism directly. Managers sort entry work by task, not by seniority, consistent with Hosseini and Lichtinger (2025) and Raymond and Dillon (2026). Expected cuts for experienced hires too contradict a simple story of seniors complementing AI. It identifies a mechanism, not the size of $$b_o$$ ([Main patterns](#main-patterns)).

**Caveat.** Answers are stated intentions about a hypothetical, from frequent AI users; large firms are overrepresented. The expectation shares are descriptive and say nothing about how many hires change.

### Bastani et al. (2026), "AI, Automation, and Fiscal Pressure"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Uppsala University and IFAU, arXiv preprint, 25 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34234). Two-sector task-based general equilibrium model with tax-funded public services, calibrated to 2023 accounts and tax data for the EU-27 and each member state; theory and calibration.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether growth driven by AI automation makes it easier or harder for European governments to pay for health care and schools.
Why it matters: Many hope AI growth will ease tight budgets. If it shifts income from taxed wages to lightly taxed profits, deficits grow instead.
How it fits the evidence: It adds a public finance channel to the debate on who captures AI gains: the state loses when the wage share falls.

**What it finds.** Automation widens the primary deficit at unchanged tax rates if public services keep pace with demand. Labour is taxed at 37.0% and capital at 19.6% in the EU-27, so a lower labour share erodes the base. In the benchmark, the automated task share rises 5 points in the market sector and 2.5 in public services. Output grows 12.3%, and the deficit rises by 0.87 points of value added, almost all from the tax base. The rise runs from 0.41 points in Luxembourg to 1.43 in Slovakia. If public services are frozen in real terms, the deficit instead falls by 1.54 points.

**Where it fits.** For the companion review's sections on aggregating task gains and on who captures the gains: it uses Acemoglu (2025) task cost savings and the Aghion, Jones and Jones (2019) Baumol logic, but finds the tax-base channel, not cost disease, drives fiscal pressure. It complements Imas and Moll (2026) and Restrepo (2025) on a falling labour share.

**Caveat.** Automation is exogenous, capital supply perfectly elastic, and unemployment is left out. Results hinge on each automated task initially saving 20% of cost and on how governments set provision.

### Levy Yeyati (2026), "Burning the Bridge: Agentification and Domestic Value Added"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Universidad Torcuato Di Tella and Inter-American Development Bank, arXiv preprint, 23 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31518). Small open economy model of firms importing foreign AI agents, with scenario maps calibrated to 2014 Brazilian and Mexican accounts; theory, not a causal design.

**In brief.**
What it explores: What happens to a middle-income country's income when local firms hand work to AI agents sold by foreign companies.
Why it matters: Each firm saves money, but the payments go abroad. The country as a whole can end up poorer.
How it fits the evidence: It warns that productivity gains at adopting firms need not become national income where AI is imported.

**What it finds.** Once wages hit workers' reservation floor, using more foreign agents in local services lowers domestic income even if agents are cheap. Real value added also falls if suppliers capture enough of the saving: above a bargaining share of about 0.15 in local services for Brazil (0.17 for Mexico). Exporters can pay for agents by selling more abroad, so their threshold is higher, about 0.66 for Brazil and 0.45 for Mexico. Using 2014 WIOD accounts, the paper maps how much export growth is needed to keep income and consumption unchanged. Cheaper agents ease the consumption requirement; lower export prices raise it.

**Where it fits.** For the companion review's section on who captures the gains: it extends Restrepo (2025) and Imas and Moll (2026) to an open economy where rents go to foreign suppliers. It sharpens Björkegren, Lagakos and VanVuren (2026) on whether AI growth reaches developing countries, adding a balance of payments constraint.

**Caveat.** Task replacement, agent costs and bargaining shares are not observed but varied as scenarios. The maps show where gains and losses occur, not where Brazil or Mexico stand today.

### Raymond and Dillon (2026), "AI-Assisted Programming and Labor Demand"
<!-- kind: paper -->

MIT and Microsoft Research, arXiv preprint, 23 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31083). Staggered difference in differences, with pre-scheduled GitHub contract reviews instrumenting Copilot adoption; 5,414 multinational firms, January 2021 to June 2025.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether large companies that gave their programmers an AI coding assistant then hired fewer programmers, and which ones.
Why it matters: Beginners gain most from AI on single tasks. If firms still hire fewer of them, the first rung of a software career narrows.
How it fits the evidence: It confirms, with a cleaner comparison than before, that firms adjust by hiring fewer juniors, not by laying people off.

**What it finds.** Adopting GitHub Copilot cuts technical hiring, mostly of juniors in code-heavy roles, with no rise in separations. GitHub's adoption and contract records are linked to Revelio employment data for 5,414 firms holding 38% of the US programming workforce, comparing firms reviewed earlier with firms not yet reviewed. A review raises adoption by 20 percentage points within a year; monthly technical hiring is then 7.6% lower. Among firms induced to adopt, quarterly technical hiring falls 29%, from about 55 hires to 39, and junior software engineer hiring about 40%. Separations and technical headcount do not change.

**Where it fits.** Section [“Firm adoption studies”](#firm-adoption-studies) gains its first adoption design that deals with selection. It confirms Hosseini and Lichtinger (2025) and Borri et al. (2026): fewer junior hires, no layoffs. Novice productivity gains (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond 2025; Dillon et al. 2025) do not raise demand for novices. Occupation-wide shocks explain at least half of the post-2022 fall in technical hiring, a warning for exposure designs.

**Caveat.** Estimates cover large GitHub customers induced to adopt, over the first year. One author works at Microsoft, which owns GitHub and supplied the adoption data.

### Jones and Tonetti (2026), "Past Automation and Future AI: How Weak Links Tame the Growth Explosion"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Stanford GSB, NBER Working Paper 36050, November 2026. [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w36050). Task-based growth accounting for the US since 1950 and a calibrated model of endogenous automation; theory and measurement, not a causal design.

**In brief.**
What it explores: How much past US growth came from machines taking over tasks, and how fast AI could speed growth up if that continues.
Why it matters: Forecasts for AI range from little change to explosive growth. This paper anchors them in seventy years of data.
How it fits the evidence: Growth does speed up, but slowly: tasks people still do hold the whole economy back for decades.

**What it finds.** Automation drove almost all past US productivity growth, yet AI speeds growth up slowly because tasks people still do are weak links. In BEA and BLS industry accounts, with language models gauging the cost share of newly automated tasks, machines' task productivity grew 4.9 percentage points a year faster than labour's in private business since 1950. Freezing automated tasks at 1950 removes 91% of automation's TFP contribution. With an elasticity across tasks of 0.2 and past patterns continuing, growth reaches 3.2% in 2050 and 4.8% in 2075; GDP per person is 15% above trend in 2050. Growth explodes around 2150, or 2050 in a computer-sector calibration.

**Where it fits.** For the companion review's sections on aggregating task gains and automating goods and services: it measures the Aghion, Jones and Jones (2019) bottleneck and finds it binding, so next-decade gains sit nearer Acemoglu (2025) than Korinek et al. (2026). Explosive growth (Davidson et al. 2026) comes late. Fernández-Villaverde (2026) argues optimal saving undoes much of the delay.

**Caveat.** The cost share of newly automated tasks comes from language model queries, an untested measure, and the elasticity drives the timing. Jones is also at Anthropic.

### Fernández-Villaverde (2026), "Saving for the Machines"
<!-- kind: paper -->

University of Pennsylvania, PIER Working Paper 26-018 (20 October 2026); NBER Working Paper 35964, November 2026. [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35964). Calibrated task-based growth model of the US with optimal saving; theory, not a causal design.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether an economy where machines can do every job would grow fast, and who would save to pay for the machines.
Why it matters: Building AI takes huge investment. If households do not save enough to pay for it, the promised boom may never come.
How it fits the evidence: Slow growth in other models may come from too little saving, not only from tasks that people alone can do.

**What it finds.** How much households save decides whether automation brings a growth boom, even when tasks are strong complements. In a model calibrated to the US in 2025, with every task automatable by 2045 and an elasticity across tasks of 0.2, growth in 2045 is 11.7% with optimal saving but 2.2% with saving fixed at its 2025 rate of 23%. Optimal saving rises to about 56% of output as machines earn more of income. The ten-year real yield jumps on impact from 5.1% to 13.6% (elasticity of one) or 9.3% (0.2). Wage earners without assets lose the equivalent of 40% to 65% of consumption.

**Where it fits.** For the companion review's sections on automating goods and services and on who captures the gains: it extends Imas and Moll (2026) to optimal saving and complementary tasks, and argues that optimal saving turns the slow acceleration of Jones and Tonetti (2026) into double-digit growth. Wages capped by the cost of machines echo Restrepo (2025).

**Caveat.** These are benchmarks, not forecasts: the automation path is assumed, and the boom rests on an infinitely lived household able to save over half of output.

### Mohnen and Gans (2026), "How AI Impacts the Quest for Knowledge: Evidence from AlphaFold2"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Ottawa and Toronto, NBER Working Paper 36045, November 2026. [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w36045). Difference in differences with a continuous treatment: each protein's AlphaFold2 prediction confidence, before and after the July 2021 release; 530,471 proteins, 2017 to 2025.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether an AI tool that predicts the shape of proteins led biologists to study new, unknown proteins, or to dig deeper into ones already known.
Why it matters: Hopes of faster growth rest on AI speeding up discovery. If AI steers scientists towards familiar ground, new knowledge may grow more slowly than hoped.
How it fits the evidence: As with jobs, AI replaces the first step: fewer first discoveries, more follow-up work on what is already known.

**What it finds.** Where AlphaFold2's predictions are more confident, experiments shift from unexplored proteins to proteins already studied. The data link 530,471 proteins to 92,918 experiments deposited in the Protein Data Bank, 2017 to 2025, comparing proteins with more and less confident predictions before and after July 2021. Moving from the bottom to the top quartile of confidence lowers the annual chance that a protein far from any known structure gets its first experiment by about 0.16 percentage points, close to half its pre-release rate of 0.35%. First structures in a group of similar proteins fall 24%; follow-up experiments rise 19%, complexes 26%, total experiments 13%.

**Where it fits.** For the companion review's section on automating ideas: AI lowers the cost of building on known results more than it extends the frontier, a channel absent from the research feedback loops of Davidson et al. (2026) and Trammell and Korinek (2026). It sharpens Lu et al. (2026) on breakthroughs exhausting themselves, and mirrors section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry): AI substitutes for the first rung.

**Caveat.** Confidence is not random: low-confidence proteins are longer and better studied. First experiments fall only in 2022 and 2023, then return near zero, and deposits count successful experiments, not attempts.

### Borri et al. (2026), "The Rise of Agents and the Labor Market"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Luiss, Dallas Fed, Rochester and Yale, arXiv preprint, 20 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27210). Stock-market exposure to AI agent use and job postings of 1,784 listed US firms; event study around Claude Code 2.0 (September 2025).

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether US companies most open to AI agents, software that carries out multi-step tasks on its own, advertised fewer jobs once agents improved.
Why it matters: Agents can take over whole chains of work, not single tasks. If firms advertise fewer junior jobs, entrants feel it first.
How it fits the evidence: It carries the finding of fewer first jobs, not layoffs, from chatbots to agents.

**What it finds.** Firms more exposed to AI agents advertised fewer jobs once agents improved, mostly junior ones. Exposure is how a firm's stock return moves with weekly agentic token use on OpenRouter (about 2% of global AI tokens); postings are 34.8 million Lightcast ads, January 2022 to July 2026. After Claude Code 2.0 (29 September 2025), firms one standard deviation more exposed beforehand saw 4.73% lower six-month posting growth, with no gap before; the panel estimate is 2.11%. Non-senior postings fell 4.54%; senior ones showed no significant change. Advertised wages held. Broad AI exposure predicts nothing once agentic exposure is included.

**Where it fits.** It extends section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry) to agents: as in Hosseini and Lichtinger (2025) and Ahn and Carollo (2026), adjustment runs through fewer junior hires. Flat advertised wages contrast with the pay cuts in Azar et al. (2026). Occupation scores (Eloundou et al. 2024) add little once firm exposure is known, consistent with Massenkoff and McCrory (2026): exposure is not use.

**Caveat.** Exposure is read from stock prices, which may also price expected demand shocks. Postings are not hires, the sample is listed firms, and OpenRouter traffic may not represent agent use inside large companies.

### Van Nieuwerburgh and Su (2026), "Clouds of Credit: Mapping the Financial Web Behind AI Compute"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Columbia Business School, NBER Working Paper 36032, November 2026. [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w36032). Network model of NVIDIA, six hyperscalers, two AI labs, two neoclouds and data-centre landlords, calibrated to their claims at 30 June 2026; theory, not a causal design.

**In brief.**
What it explores: How the trillions borrowed and promised to build AI data centres tie chipmakers, cloud firms, AI labs and lenders together, and who loses if AI disappoints.
Why it matters: Growth from AI needs this buildout. If it is too big or too indebted, a disappointment could hit savers and lenders hard.
How it fits the evidence: It adds the money side to growth forecasts: firms may build about half again more than is best for society.

**What it finds.** Firms overbuild AI compute, and debt makes the network fragile. In equilibrium the network adds 277.5 GW of compute for &#36;6.30 trillion of capital spending, 55% above the planner's 178.9 GW, because each firm ignores how its capacity cuts rivals' revenue. Debt finances about 59% of the expansion; lender debt rises from 5.9% to 26.3% of equity. If AI productivity turns out very low (a state given 25% probability), outside investors lose &#36;16.87 trillion, against &#36;8.8 trillion without the buildout; outside lenders lose about 42% of their stake. Blocking payment spillovers between firms cuts losses by about 30%.

**Where it fits.** For the companion review's section on aggregating task gains: scenarios such as Korinek et al. (2026) take the compute path as given; here competing firms choose it and overshoot. It calibrates the AI investment race of Rungcharoenkitkul (2026) on measured contracts and extends Van Nieuwerburgh (2026). It describes the downside of the buildout, not a growth forecast.

**Caveat.** Productivity states, their probabilities and many private-firm exposures are assumptions. Market values discipline the calibration, so any mispricing passes into the results, and the model has one period with no feedback to the wider economy.

### Bekkerman et al. (2026), "Large Language Models Have Not Yet Transformed Science"
<!-- kind: paper -->

New Jersey Institute of Technology, arXiv preprint, 17 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22975). Trend breaks at 2022 in 2.5 million papers, 2015 to 2025; descriptive, outside the review's causal evidence standard; context for AI and growth.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether chatbots have changed how science is written, organised and produced since late 2022, across 2.5 million research papers.
Why it matters: Forecasts of fast AI-led growth rest on AI speeding up research. This checks whether whole fields have shifted yet.
How it fits the evidence: As with jobs, the big picture barely moves: some words changed, but most long-run trends carried on.

**What it finds.** Beyond word choice, science after 2022 follows its earlier trends with few detectable breaks. The data are 2.5 million full-text papers in computer science, mathematics and physics (arXiv) and health and life sciences (PubMed Central). Of 14,484 common word roots, 796 surge after 2022, 71 rise then fall ("delve" peaks in 2024) and 199 decline. Lexical diversity shows no slope change in any field, and the density of scientific terms keeps rising. Team sizes keep rising as before, except in Asian computer science. Paper counts grow faster after 2022 in computer science and physics; the 2023 jump in biomedicine follows a COVID-era dip.

**Where it fits.** For the companion review's section on automating ideas: Hermle et al. (2026) find heavy research use of AI and project up to a doubling of output; here, three years in, field-level practice follows pre-2022 trends, except paper counts. As for jobs (Humlum and Vestergaard 2025), task gains have not reached aggregates, closer to Aghion, Jones and Jones (2019) than to Davidson et al.

**Caveat.** Writing traits and paper counts are weak proxies for ideas or quality. There is no comparison group, and three post-2022 years are short and blurred by the COVID rebound.

### Ahn and Carollo (2026), "Artificial Intelligence and Labor Market Reallocation"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Federal Reserve Board staff paper dated 16 July 2026, posted by the San Francisco Fed on 17 November 2026. [Paper](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/board-of-governors/2026/11/artificial-intelligence-labor-market-reallocation/). US worker flows by AI exposure and adoption, before and after late 2022; group comparisons, not a causal design.

**In brief.**
What it explores: How AI has changed Americans' chances of finding a job, losing one or changing jobs since ChatGPT arrived.
Why it matters: If AI slows hiring without layoffs, unemployment can creep up quietly; central banks need to see it early.
How it fits the evidence: It confirms AI works through fewer hires, not more firings, and puts a first number on unemployment.

**What it finds.** Since late 2022, US workers whose jobs are most open to AI, in industries that adopt it, find work more slowly; job loss barely moved. The authors link Current Population Survey records (2012 to May 2026) to OpenAI- and Anthropic-based exposure scores and to Lightcast's share of postings asking for AI skills. Unemployment in this high-exposure, high-adoption group rose by slightly under 1 percentage point from end-2022 to May 2026, against 0.4 points for the low-exposure, low-adoption group. Job-to-job moves fell, switching activities within the same job rose, and exposed workers' wage-growth premium faded. The natural rate of unemployment rose by about 0.2 points, with wide uncertainty.

**Where it fits.** It extends section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry) from young entrants to all US job seekers: as in Cline and Kaymak (2026) and Hosseini and Lichtinger (2025), adjustment runs through hiring, not separations. Splitting exposure by adoption separates potential from use, as in Massenkoff and McCrory (2026). Rising within-job task switching fits section [“Does reorganisation create new tasks?”](#does-reorganisation-create-new-tasks).

**Caveat.** Adoption is an industry measure from postings, and the late-2022 break coincides with rate rises and the cooling of tech hiring. Classing the unemployed by last occupation leaves out first-job seekers.

### Chow et al. (2026), "AI-Induced Social Erosion: Using LLMs for Work Tasks Reduces Help-Seeking Intentions Towards Colleagues"
<!-- kind: paper -->

NYU, Illinois and Carnegie Mellon, arXiv preprint, 18 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25269). Randomised vignette experiment on 248 US knowledge workers; outcomes are stated intentions, not observed behaviour.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether office workers who get help from AI on a task then turn to their colleagues less.
Why it matters: Asking colleagues is how newcomers learn and how teams bond; if AI takes those questions, both may weaken.
How it fits the evidence: A one-firm study found AI users met more colleagues; this one measures intentions, not actions.

**What it finds.** Imagining a task done with AI lowered workers' stated wish to ask a colleague for help. 248 US knowledge workers who use AI weekly each rated ten hypothetical scenarios (2,444 ratings) on 1 to 5 scales. With AI, help-seeking fell by 0.24 points and felt connection to the colleague by 0.25 points, relative to the same tasks without AI. The drop was largest for brainstorming (0.41 points) and proofing (0.26), and not significant for reassurance and search. Using AI in a workspace colleagues can see, rather than privately, raised help-seeking by 0.31 points. Delegating the task to AI, rather than being assisted, made no difference.

**Where it fits.** It informs section [“Who holds knowledge?”](#who-holds-knowledge): if AI answers the questions workers used to take to colleagues, it replaces the help layer of the Garicano (2000) hierarchy, as in Ide and Talamàs (2025). It contrasts with Soda, Iorio and Rizzo (2026), where Copilot adopters collaborated with more colleagues. The shared-workspace effect fits the review's point that organisation decides the direction.

**Caveat.** Hypothetical scenarios measure intentions on the day, not behaviour over months. The sample is self-selected online workers who already trust AI, and effects are a fraction of a point on a five-point scale.

### Dueñas and Mandel (2026), "Structural economic change under the digital transformation"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Sant'Anna Pisa and Paris School of Economics, arXiv preprint, 19 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26338). Agent-based model of 163 industries in 49 economies, calibrated to 2022 input-output data; simulation, not a causal design.

**In brief.**
What it explores: What happens worldwide if the industries supplying computing, software and telecoms become a third more productive over ten years.
Why it matters: AI growth forecasts often stop at national output; who must change industry, and whether they can, decides who pays.
How it fits the evidence: It finds output gains of a few per cent and modest but lasting pressure on workers in industries that pay less.

**What it finds.** A 35% rise in digital suppliers' productivity (computing, telecoms, electronics, ICT services) over ten years raises GDP everywhere: about 3% in the EU, close to 6% in China. Gross output gains exceed value-added gains; value added falls in some sectors, such as agriculture and food in Mexico, India and Canada. When digital inputs substitute directly for capital and labour in the 49 industries OECD taxonomies rank most AI- or digital-exposed, digital value added rises by 48% (UK) to just over 70% (China). Labour moves into digital industries, largely from services. Without mobility, reallocation gaps reach 1.0 to 1.3% of employment in Germany and 0.5 to 0.7% in the US.

**Where it fits.** For the companion review's section on aggregating task gains, it adds propagation through production networks: gains show up in gross output more than in value added, a channel Acemoglu (2025) and Korinek et al. (2026) abstract from. Persistent wage gaps across industries bear on whether displaced workers can move, as in Hyman et al. (2025).

**Caveat.** The productivity shock is assumed, not estimated, and covers all digital suppliers rather than AI alone. Substitution elasticities are set, not measured, and labour gaps are wage-based pressures, not unemployment.

### Zhang, Esposito and Yang (2026), "AI, Specialization, and the Organization of Knowledge Work: Evidence from Legal Services"
<!-- kind: paper -->

micro1 (an AI data and benchmark firm) and Northeastern University, working paper on arXiv, 17 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23354). Difference in differences across 89 federal court districts, civil cases against prisoner petitions; law-firm and employment evidence is descriptive.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether cheaper legal know-how from AI leads people and companies to do legal work themselves, or moves work between law firms.
Why it matters: Professional services are where AI most promises cheap expertise; who keeps the work decides who gains.
How it fits the evidence: More people now go to court without a lawyer, yet companies have not hired more in-house lawyers, and top firms charge more.

**What it finds.** Self-representation rose but paid legal work did not shrink. Across 89 US federal districts, the share of non-prisoner civil cases with a self-represented party rose 6.6 percentage points more than for prisoner petitions between fiscal 2022 and 2025, or 5.7 points after removing an earlier trend. It reached 16.8% in fiscal 2025, against 9.5 to 12.1% for two decades, rising most in drafting-heavy civil rights cases (26% to 38%). Household legal spending stayed above its 2010 to 2019 trend. The in-house share of private-sector lawyers was 29.0% in 2024, against 28.7% in 2019. In 2025 Am Law 100 revenue grew 13% while billable hours rose 0.1%.

**Where it fits.** It informs section [“Who holds knowledge?”](#who-holds-knowledge): as in Garicano (2000), cheaper knowledge lets clients solve more problems themselves, but here households, not firms, take work in-house. Stable demand for premium firms favours scale over substitution in [Main patterns](#main-patterns). It adds a sector to Esposito and Zhang (2026), where adjustment shows up first as reallocation rather than job creation.

**Caveat.** The authors work for micro1, which makes legal-AI benchmarks cited in the paper. Prisoner petitions are an imperfect comparison, and the law-firm patterns predate generative AI.

### Xu, Medappa and Tunc (2026), "Does the AI Reviewer Alleviate or Aggravate the Maintenance Burden? Evidence from GitHub Copilot Reviewer"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Tilburg University, working paper on arXiv, 17 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22793). Staggered difference in differences comparing GitHub maintainers who adopted an AI code reviewer with those not yet adopting; survival models for time to merge.

**In brief.**
What it explores: What happens to the people who check code when an AI reviewer joins open-source software projects.
Why it matters: As AI makes work cheaper to produce, checking it becomes the bottleneck.
How it fits the evidence: AI sped up each review but drew more submissions, so the human checkers' workload rose rather than fell.

**What it finds.** The AI reviewer raised maintainers' workload. Adopters handled about 10% more pull requests (proposed code changes) and 14% more review comments per request than maintainers who had not yet adopted. Human requests rose 8% and bot requests 5%; the human surge faded after a month and fell below the comparison group, while bot requests kept rising. Human requests shrank (14.1% fewer added lines, 33.8% fewer files). Data: 3,937 Python repositories, 689 adopting and 1,902 other maintainers, about 975,000 requests, monthly March 2024 to February 2026 (tool launched February 2025). AI-reviewed human requests merged faster (merge rate 4.2% higher), bot requests slower (21.5% lower), than requests without AI review.

**Where it fits.** It informs section [“Does reorganisation create new tasks?”](#does-reorganisation-create-new-tasks): handing screening to AI moved the bottleneck to human verification instead of freeing time, as with the unchanged meeting time in Dillon et al. (2025). It extends Hoffmann et al. (2025), where a coding assistant moved developers away from coordination. More submissions after cheaper review echo the scale force in [Main patterns](#main-patterns).

**Caveat.** Maintainers chose to adopt, perhaps when expecting more activity; merge-time comparisons are descriptive. The sample is popular Python open-source projects, not paid teams.

### Björkegren, Lagakos and VanVuren (2026), "Will AI Growth Reach the Developing World?"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Columbia, Boston University and Vanderbilt, NBER Working Paper 36035, November 2026. [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w36035). Two-country growth model with a world market for AI, simulated over 25 years; theory and calibration, not a causal design.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether fast progress in AI will lift poor countries as much as rich ones, or leave them further behind.
Why it matters: Most AI growth forecasts cover only rich economies, home to a minority of the world's people.
How it fits the evidence: It takes US growth scenarios to a world where rich countries bid up the price of AI.

**What it finds.** AI raises developing-economy incomes, but advanced-economy incomes more. With AI productivity growing 15% or 50% a year, the income gap widens by at least 40% in about half of scenarios. If AI substitutes for labour and grows 50% a year, every draw diverges without bound; if it complements labour, the typical gap grows 2.4-fold. Missing complementary skills and rich-country demand for AI drive this. An AI-use subsidy returns 11 cents per dollar; closing 1% of the productivity gap yearly lets the poor economy outgrow the rich one under modest AI growth. Halting consumption divergence needs transfers of 1 to 15% of advanced-economy GDP, against 0.22% for US aid in 2024.

**Where it fits.** It extends the companion review's task-gain scenarios (Korinek et al. 2026) and its section on who captures the gains (Restrepo 2025) to two economies sharing one AI market. Satiated demand for AI-intensive output acts like the weak links of Jones and Tonetti (2026). Wage losses for skilled workers whose tasks AI does match section [“Exposure designs: wages”](#exposure-designs-wages).

**Caveat.** Results hinge on unmeasured substitution and satiation parameters; 95% bands are ten times wider than the median path. Björkegren has received research funding from AI companies.

### Esposito and Zhang (2026), "AI and the Two Margins of Work: Why Job Losses Show Up Before Job Creation"
<!-- kind: paper -->

micro1 and Northeastern University, working paper on arXiv, 12 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17530). Calibrated model plus descriptive industry comparisons in Census and BLS job-flow data; not a causal design, reported as theory and context.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether jobs lost inside existing firms show up in the statistics sooner than jobs created by new firms and new products.
Why it matters: If new work is counted late, early figures will make AI look more destructive than it is.
How it fits the evidence: It agrees that total jobs have not moved yet, and gives a reason why losses would appear first.

**What it finds.** In the model, AI cuts aggregate hours only if products take time to re-scale: a sudden efficiency gain cuts hired hours by 4.6% at once, while the same gain spread over seven years, like actual adoption, gives a dip of about a quarter of a percent. In the computing era (Census data, 1982 to 1998), entering firms added 2.78 million payroll jobs a year while incumbents shed about 0.96 million net, and firms born from 1978 held 42% of payroll by 1998. In BLS data for 72 industries, 2022Q4 to 2026Q1, incumbent losses rise by 1.1 points of 2019 employment per standard deviation of language-model exposure, standard error 1.9.

**Where it fits.** It informs section [“Why these patterns: theory”](#why-these-patterns-theory) and the scale term $$\eta_o-\sigma_o$$ in [Main patterns](#main-patterns): substitution acts at once, scale arrives later with new firms. It matches flat totals in Humlum and Vestergaard (2025), and its prediction that effects show first in hiring into exposed jobs fits Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) and Hosseini and Lichtinger (2025).

**Caveat.** As the authors note, the industry null cannot tell slow diffusion from no effect. Both authors work at micro1, an AI company.

### Fleck et al. (2026), "AI Implementation at Work: Worker Preferences for Task Changes"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Maastricht University, IZA Discussion Paper 19076, November 2026. [Paper](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/19076/ai-implementation-at-work-worker-preferences-for-task-changes). Discrete choice experiment (workers pick between hypothetical jobs) in one manufacturing firm; stated preferences, not observed outcomes, reported as context.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether factory inspectors prefer AI that helps them decide, or AI that takes over the easy cases and leaves them only the hard ones.
Why it matters: How a firm brings in AI shapes whether workers accept it and what their jobs become.
How it fits the evidence: It gives workers' own view on the choice between AI that helps and AI that replaces.

**What it finds.** Workers value decision-support AI like a 3% pay rise, and AI that takes over easy tasks hardly at all. The sample is 423 quality control operators of a multinational automotive manufacturer, mostly in Mexico and Romania (4,992 choices between pairs of jobs). Against the job without AI (50 to 100 easy images a minute), decision-support AI (30 to 60 images a minute, with pop-up flags) raises a job's choice probability by 10.4 points, autonomous AI (10 to 30 hard images an hour) by 3.0 points. Willingness to pay is 2.8% and 0.7% (marginally significant) of salary. Workers who found the support AI useful liked the autonomous stage less.

**Where it fits.** It informs section [“Organisation: who decides how AI is used”](#organisation-who-decides-how-ai-is-used): workers prefer what Acemoglu et al. (2026) call augmenting technology over automation, and value it like a small raise. It points to a cost of stepwise rollouts that move from augmentation to automation, a margin firm adoption studies do not observe.

**Caveat.** Choices are hypothetical, in one firm and one occupation, among mostly female workers with intermediate education. The firm helped design the choices, which may frame how AI was presented.
