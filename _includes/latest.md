New papers on AI and labour markets and AI and growth, newest first, each placed against the review below. Every figure is checked against the paper itself.

### Carollo et al. (2026), "Artificial Intelligence and the U.S. Economy: Current Evidence"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Federal Reserve Board, Finance and Economics Discussion Series 2026-074, 12 November 2026. [Paper](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/artificial-intelligence-and-the-us-economy-current-evidence.htm). Staff review of US data and studies on AI investment, adoption, prices, jobs and productivity; descriptive, reported as context.

**In brief.**
What it explores: What AI has done to the US economy so far: data-centre spending, use at work, prices, jobs and productivity.
Why it matters: It is Federal Reserve staff's own reading of the evidence, and it shapes how central bankers judge AI.
How it fits the evidence: AI shows up in spending and in fewer first jobs, not yet in total jobs or in productivity.

**What it finds.** AI is visible in investment and some prices, barely in jobs and productivity. In the authors' middle scenario, measured data-centre investment rises from about &#36;100 billion in 2024 to over &#36;880 billion by end-2027, above 2% of GDP, with about half of it imported. Annualised revenue of OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind reached at least &#36;107 billion in 2026Q3, about 19% of software gross output. Over 40% of workers use AI at work, but only 10% daily. Core PCE categories at the 90th percentile of data-centre exposure saw about 5 points more inflation since January 2025 than the median one. The recent productivity pickup mostly predates generative AI.

**Where it fits.** For growth, it supports the companion review's "what the data show so far": AI enters GDP through investment before productivity, with Jones and Tonetti (2026) weak links as a brake. For labour, it reads the entry evidence as Tucker (2026) and Hosseini and Lichtinger (2025) do: fewer young hires, small aggregate effects (section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry)).

**Caveat.** A synthesis, not new estimates. Most of the analysis was completed in June 2026, and the revenue figures rest on unaudited company statements.

### Major (2026), "Monitoring Early Effects of AI on the Labour Market"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Reserve Bank of Australia, RBA Insight 2026-09, 9 November 2026. [Paper](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/insights/australian-economy/2026/monitoring-early-effects-of-ai-on-the-labour-market.html). Labour-market indicators by occupational AI exposure and age against a November 2022 benchmark; descriptive monitoring, reported as context.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether Australians in the jobs most open to AI, and young people, have fared worse since ChatGPT arrived.
Why it matters: A central bank needs early warning if AI starts to push up unemployment, before it shows in national totals.
How it fits the evidence: It finds the faint pattern seen elsewhere: little change overall, weaker outcomes for young workers in jobs open to AI.

**What it finds.** There is little sign yet that AI has disrupted Australia's labour market, with tentative signs of weaker outcomes for young workers in the most exposed occupations. Occupations are ranked into employment-weighted quintiles by Jobs and Skills Australia's automation exposure measure (from Gmyrek et al. 2023). Job ads and Labour Force Survey data, complete to February 2026, are compared with November 2022. New top-quintile ads slowed a little more, led by clerks, programmers, ICT analysts, call-centre workers and receptionists. Job-finding and job-loss rates show little gap by exposure. Employment and hours grew a little less in exposed occupations, and within them young workers did worse. Results are in charts only.

**Where it fits.** It adds Australia to section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry), next to the US, Sweden, the UK and France. Unlike Cline and Kaymak (2026) for the US, it finds no gap in job-finding rates by exposure. Like Mehdi and Frenette (2026) for Canada, it is an official monitoring exercise, not an estimate of $$b_o$$ ([Main patterns](#main-patterns)).

**Caveat.** Exposure measures potential, not actual use. The 2022 benchmark was an exceptionally tight youth labour market, so later weakness may be cyclical, and post-pandemic remote work hits the same groups.

### Cline and Kaymak (2026), "Job Finding and Unemployment in the Age of AI"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, Economic Commentary 2026-26, 9 November 2026. [Paper](https://www.clevelandfed.org/publications/economic-commentary/2026/ec-202626-job-finding-and-unemployment-in-the-age-of-ai). Worker flows by occupational AI exposure, 2015–2019 against 2024–2026, seasonal controls only, no pre-trend test; descriptive, reported as context.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether Americans whose work AI can do are losing jobs, or struggling to find new ones.
Why it matters: Losing a job and failing to find one call for different help: protecting people in work, or supporting job seekers.
How it fits the evidence: It supports the view that AI means fewer hires, not more layoffs, with young people hit hardest.

**What it finds.** In the most AI-exposed US occupations, unemployment rose because the unemployed took longer to find work, not because more workers lost jobs. Using linked monthly Current Population Survey records for 23 to 54 year olds, the authors rank 923 occupations into 20 exposure groups and compare 2015–2019 with May 2025 to April 2026 (flows from January 2024). Unemployment rose about 1 percentage point more in the most than the least exposed group. The monthly job-finding rate, about 34% on average, fell about 6 points more, lengthening spells from about 10.5 to over 13 weeks. The unemployment gap is about 2 points at ages 23 to 30.

**Where it fits.** It carries the entry margin of section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry) to the unemployed: as in Tucker (2026) and Bergeaud, Bertheau, et al. (2026), hiring falls while separations do not, and the rise echoes Kläui and Siegenthaler (2025). It qualifies Imas and Schaal (2026), who see no unemployment effect (section [“Evaluation”](#evaluation)). The flatter usage-based slope fits a productivity gain $$\pi_o$$ offsetting substitution ([Main patterns](#main-patterns)).

**Caveat.** The 2015–2019 baseline cannot separate AI from the decline of exposed jobs from early 2022 (Frank et al. 2026). Classing the unemployed by last occupation leaves out first-job seekers.

### Diener et al. (2026), "Today's Work, Tomorrow's Workforce: Incentivizing Learning in AI-Assisted Work"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Karlsruhe Institute of Technology and IBM Research, arXiv preprint, 4 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06346). Dynamic model of workers choosing between accepting an AI's answer and solving tasks themselves; theory with illustrative numbers, no causal estimates.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether workers who can accept an AI's answer still practise enough to learn their job, or keep their skills.
Why it matters: Juniors have always learned by doing routine work. If AI does it, someone must decide who trains the next experts.
How it fits the evidence: It offers a reason why firms may stop training juniors, and prices what keeping them learning would cost.

**What it finds.** Relying on AI can trap novices at low skill and let experts lose theirs, while output quality stays at the AI's level, so the loss goes unseen. Solving by hand costs effort but builds skill; accepting saves effort and lets skill decay. Even forward-looking workers stay trapped when learning pays too late. In the authors' code-review example, a novice who finds 40% of bugs, beside an AI that finds 62%, never practises. Paying for correct unaided answers, at first 0.27 of a bug's value per task and falling with skill, carries the novice to 70%, after which payments stop.

**Where it fits.** It formalises the training channel in section [“Why these patterns: theory”](#why-these-patterns-theory): as in Garicano and Rayo (2025), AI removes the routine work through which juniors paid for their training. It adds expert deskilling and an explicit price for practice, a choice that sits with employers (section [“Organisation: who decides how AI is used”](#organisation-who-decides-how-ai-is-used)). It does not test the junior hiring falls of Hosseini and Lichtinger (2025).

**Caveat.** One worker, one task family and chosen parameters, not estimates. Skill grows only through unaided work; if engaged use of AI also teaches, the trap weakens, as the authors note.

### Lu et al. (2026), "Stronger artificial intelligence breakthroughs hasten their own exhaustion"
<!-- kind: paper -->

University of Chicago Knowledge Lab and Tsinghua University, arXiv preprint, 19 October 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27296). Descriptive analysis of 36 years of computer science papers, career moves and peer reviews, with an illustrative simulation; outside the causal evidence standard, context for AI and growth.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether pouring more researchers and money into AI research produces more new ideas, or fewer different ones.
Why it matters: Forecasts of very fast growth assume AI research speeds up as effort grows. Crowding onto one approach could slow it instead.
How it fits the evidence: It adds a brake to growth forecasts: ideas get harder to find partly because everyone chases the same one.

**What it finds.** Since the Transformer in 2017, computer science has grown while its attention narrowed, and each AI breakthrough was followed by faster exhaustion of its ideas. Across 7.18 million papers from 1990 to 2025, inequality of attention across keywords (a Gini coefficient) rose from 0.26 to 0.33 within eight years of 2017, after decades without change. Of 918,862 moves between subfields, 53.81% ended in AI, mostly by mid-career researchers. In 73,089 peer reviews, 2017 to 2025, reviewer confidence fell from 3.9 to 2.9 out of 5. Active ideas fell below their ceiling modestly after AlexNet, severely after the Transformer.

**Where it fits.** It bears on the companion review's section on automating ideas. Growth models where ideas get harder to find (Aghion, Jones and Jones 2019) treat difficulty as a property of the idea space; here it also depends on how researchers spread their attention. That weakens the research feedback loops behind explosive growth in Davidson et al., in line with the slower paths of Rungcharoenkitkul (2026).

**Caveat.** Nouns in abstracts stand in for ideas, and the post-2022 window is short. The paper does not link narrowing attention to output; the growth reading is ours.

### Soda, Iorio and Rizzo (2026), "The Effects of Generative AI on Collaboration and Social Networks Among Knowledge Workers"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Bocconi University and SDA Bocconi, arXiv preprint, 23 October 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32461). Staggered difference in differences on logged collaboration data during a staged Microsoft Copilot rollout in one firm, checked with a shift-share instrument.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether office workers who start using an AI assistant talk to their colleagues less, or more.
Why it matters: A common fear is that AI makes work lonelier and cuts the everyday contact through which people learn from each other.
How it fits the evidence: It adds a social side to studies of AI and office work. Contact with colleagues rose rather than fell.

**What it finds.** Workers who adopted Copilot spent more time collaborating, and with more colleagues. The data are Microsoft Viva Insights logs on 11,868 knowledge workers in one unit of a multinational consumer-products firm, September 2024 to September 2025: 1,992 sustained adopters against 9,876 never-adopters, with no gap before adoption. In the first three months after adoption, collaboration hours a worker initiated rose by 1.4 a month relative to never-adopters, mostly in meetings and calls. Adopters worked with about 2.2 more distinct colleagues a month, their networks became less clustered, and they gave more of their time to colleagues they already knew well.

**Where it fits.** It informs section [“Who holds knowledge?”](#who-holds-knowledge): AI output still has to be checked and accepted by colleagues, so the assistant adds consultation rather than replacing it, unlike the shift to remote work in Yang et al. (2022). It nuances Dillon et al. (2025), where Copilot saves time within existing jobs: part of that time may go to coordination.

**Caveat.** Workers partly chose when to start using the tool, and follow-up covers three months in one firm. Logs show who meets whom, not what they discuss or whether work improved.

### Rungcharoenkitkul (2026), "Transformative AI and Demand Bottlenecks"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Bank for International Settlements, BIS Working Paper No. 1381, 10 October 2026. [Paper](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-1381-transformative-ai-and-demand-bottlenecks). Calibrated endogenous growth model of automation and innovation; theory, no causal estimates.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether AI that automates ever more work could stall itself, because building machines leaves less consumer spending to pay for inventions.
Why it matters: Some forecasts see growth of 13 to 18% a year within two decades. This asks who pays for the research.
How it fits the evidence: It sits at the cautious end of growth forecasts and predicts lower wages than without AI for decades.

**What it finds.** In the central calibration, AI raises growth briefly, then lowers it: trend growth settles 0.41 percentage points below the pre-AI 2%. Automation requires building machines, which shrinks the consumer spending that pays innovators; research stops paying and automation stalls, with labour's task share at 48% against 60% before. Output growth peaks just under 2.4% about 15 years after activation, and wages are 14.5% below the no-AI path after 20 years. The most optimistic scenario reaches 5.5% growth by year 20, against 13% in Jones and Tonetti (2026) and 18% in Korinek and Suh (2024). Trend growth ends below 2% in all 32 parameter combinations tested.

**Where it fits.** It adds a demand-side limit to the companion review's section on automating goods and services: beyond weak links (Aghion, Jones and Jones 2019; Jones and Tonetti 2026), growth can stall when innovation is paid from consumption. It projects far less than Korinek et al. (2026) because research responds to rents. Falling wages imply a high $$\sigma_o$$ ([Main patterns](#main-patterns)). Research subsidies offset the shortfall; redistribution does not.

**Caveat.** AI's arrival rate is inferred from productivity estimates of 0.06 to 1.5 points a year; across tested values the shortfall ranges from 0.02 to 1.83 points. The result rests on rents earned only from consumers.

### Conlon and Schwardmann (2026), "Creative destruction: Human-AI competition in an experimental market"
<!-- kind: paper -->

Ohio State University and Carnegie Mellon University, arXiv preprint, 12 October 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18921). Three preregistered online experiments with paid choices; who designed each game is revealed to randomly chosen buyers. A lab market, not labour market data.

**In brief.**
What it explores: Whether people directing an AI coding tool design better games than the AI alone, and whether buyers pay more for human-made games.
Why it matters: Two hopes for keeping people in production are human taste and buyers' preference for human work. This tests both.
How it fits the evidence: It adds the buyer's side to studies of AI and creative work. Neither hope holds here.

**What it finds.** Games designed by an AI alone sold for more than games designed by people directing the same coding agent, and telling buyers who made a game changed nothing. Among 296 US participants who both designed and bought games, buyers paid &#36;0.47 on average to play an AI-designed game against &#36;0.37 for a human-designed one, a gap of 0.31 standard deviations. With 549 separate buyers, games by 180 creative professionals scored 0.16 standard deviations below AI games. Producers expected to beat the AI by 9 to 17 cents. Disclosing the designer left the AI premium unchanged: 6.8 cents blind against 6.0 cents disclosed.

**Where it fits.** It tests the new task channel of Acemoglu et al. (2026) (section [“Organisation: who decides how AI is used”](#organisation-who-decides-how-ai-is-used)): directing AI gave people no edge in taste and a human label created no demand, suggesting a high $$\sigma_o$$ in creative design ([Main patterns](#main-patterns)). Producers took pride in the work, a loss GDP does not record, the theme of Imas and Moll (2026) (companion review).

**Caveat.** Design took minutes, prices were cents and play lasted about a minute. The AI had one untested prompt, which if anything understates its edge.

### Barwick et al. (2026), "AI as Shared Business Infrastructure: Firm Growth and the Distribution of Gains"
<!-- kind: paper -->

NBER Working Paper 36003, 9 November 2026. [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w36003). Matched difference in differences on a Chinese e-commerce platform's records of small sellers, checked with instrumental variables.

**What it finds.** Small online shops in China that adopted their platform's built-in AI assistant raised monthly sales by about half, and the smallest shops gained most. The data cover sellers with pre-launch annual sales of at most RMB 5 million, October 2023 to July 2025: 148,770 adopters against 77,347 similar non-adopters. Sales rose 51.6% and orders 47.0% relative to matched non-adopters, with no gap before adoption. Sales rose 61.5% in the bottom size quarter against 10.3% in the top. Adopters posted 62.6% more product images, while customer-service staff time per order fell 5.6%. Product categories where the tools mattered more saw more sales, entry and new products.

**Where it fits.** It extends section [“Firm adoption studies”](#firm-adoption-studies): like Kharazian et al. (2026), adopters grow, a scale effect, but adoption is measured from actual use and among very small firms. The fall in staff time per order is a field reading of $$\pi_o$$ ([Main patterns](#main-patterns)). Headcount is not measured, so it cannot say whether growth offsets the losses Bonin et al. (2026) find in Danish firms under 100 employees.

**Caveat.** Sellers chose to adopt, at RMB 99 a month after a free month, so keener sellers may select in; the instrumental-variable estimates are larger still. The unnamed platform sells the tool and supplied the data.

### Zhao et al. (2026), "AI Exposure and the Changing Responsibilities in Job Postings"

Microsoft, Microsoft Research and Stanford University, arXiv preprint, 6 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09870). Descriptive comparison of job postings in 2023 and 2025, no control group; outside the review's causal evidence standard, reported as context.

**What it finds.** Large US employers list AI-exposed work activities a little less often in their postings, and increasingly describe them as done with AI. In 50,000 postings from 33 large firms, an activity one standard deviation more exposed lost 0.19 to 0.24 percentage points more of its presence between 2023 and 2025, a small share of all listed activities. Of 57 activities whose presence fell, 55 were tied to AI in more postings. Postings mentioning AI rose from 5.3% to 8.8%.

**Where it fits.** Klein Teeselink and Carey (2026) find fewer postings in exposed occupations (section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry)); here exposed activities shrink only mildly within the jobs still advertised, so the demand shift runs more through which jobs are posted than through what each contains. Employers rewrite exposed activities around AI use, a stated-demand counterpart to the new tasks Dillon et al. (2025) did not find (section [“Does reorganisation create new tasks?”](#does-reorganisation-create-new-tasks)).

**Caveat.** Postings are partly signalling and show neither who is hired nor what workers do. Four of the five authors work at Microsoft, one of the 33 firms.

### Hermle et al. (2026), "AI for Scientific Discovery: How Researchers Use Claude"

The Anthropic Institute, technical report, 5 November 2026. [Paper](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-researchers-use-claude). Descriptive usage data, a survey and a calibrated ideas production function; outside the causal standard, context for AI and growth.

**What it finds.** Scientists use AI where it works best, and a calibrated model says current AI could at most double research output, with physical work capping larger gains. Across 207,049 research sessions on Claude and Claude Code (August to September 2026), data analysis takes 33.7% and 48.1% of sessions against 27.6% of researcher time; ideation takes 4.2% and 2.1% against 7.1%. Fed into an ideas production function with an elasticity of substitution of one half, current capabilities raise output 1.35 times in the arts to 2.02 times in statistics for a fixed budget. With all non-physical steps automated, fields with little lab work reach 15 to 16 times, lab fields 3 to 4 times.

**Where it fits.** Puts field-level numbers on the input side of the research-automation models of Davidson et al. and Trammell and Korinek (companion review): physical steps are the Baumol bottleneck. Use rises steeply with task capability, so measured exposure does predict where adoption goes, unlike the economy-wide gap in Massenkoff and McCrory (2026) (section [“Key concepts and definitions”](#key-concepts-and-definitions)).

**Caveat.** The authors study their employer's product over two months of consumer plans; the scenarios rest on an assumed AI cost and a model-rated capability score, not measured output.

### Bank of England (2026), "Monetary Policy Report: November 2026"

Bank of England, Monetary Policy Committee, 5 November 2026. [Paper](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/monetary-policy-report/2026/november-2026). Growth accounting with an industry proxy for AI activity (Box C) and firm survey estimates (Box D); descriptive, context for AI and growth.

**What it finds.** AI is lifting UK growth through the industries that build and sell it, while its effect on productivity elsewhere is still small. After trend adjustment, novel AI-related activity in publishing, computer programming and information services accounts for just under a fifth of UK GDP growth since the start of 2026. Firms in the Decision Maker Panel estimate that AI raised their productivity by about 0.5% over three years, which the Bank reads as 0.1 to 0.2 percentage points a year, with close to zero effect on their employment so far. Firms expect 0.9 points a year over the next three years, half of it through slower employment growth.

**Where it fits.** An official number on the pattern in Strauss, Romei and Fleming (2026) below: growth comes from producing AI, not yet from using it. The 0.1 to 0.2 points sit near Acemoglu (2025), the firms' forecast nearer Korinek et al. (2026) (companion review). Fewer hires without more firings confirm adjustment through entry (section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry)), and near-zero firm employment effects match Yotzov et al. (2026).

**Caveat.** The proxy industries do much non-AI work, so "a fifth" is an order of magnitude, and the productivity and employment figures are firms' own estimates. The end of labour hoarding and higher labour costs also cut hiring and raise measured productivity.

### Mehdi and Frenette (2026), "Hiring and job separation rates in the era of generative artificial intelligence: Early evidence"

Statistics Canada, Economic and Social Reports, 28 October 2026. [Paper](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/36-28-0001/2026010/article/00003-eng.htm). Raw hiring and separation rates by occupational AI exposure, before and after ChatGPT, no controls; outside the causal standard, context.

**What it finds.** In Canada, hiring of young workers has fallen since ChatGPT, but by as much or more in jobs little exposed to AI as in exposed ones. Comparing the six months to November 2022 with the six months to August 2026, the monthly hiring rate of 15 to 29 year olds fell from 3.2% to 3.0% in exposed jobs where AI is more likely to replace workers, from 2.9% to 2.5% in exposed jobs where it is more likely to help them, and from 4.0% to 3.3% in low-exposure jobs. In coding jobs it fell from 2.5% to 1.9%, but that decline began in early 2022. Youth separation rates stayed within their historical range.

**Where it fits.** Bergeaud, Bertheau, et al. (2026) in France and Tucker (2026) in the US find young hires falling more in exposed jobs; Canada shows no such gap, siding with the total-employment designs of Kauhanen and Rouvinen (2025) and Facius and Iacono (2026) (section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry)). Flat separations confirm the no-layoffs half of the review's margin-of-adjustment claim ([Main patterns](#main-patterns)), and the pre-ChatGPT fall in coding hires extends the point in section [“Evaluation”](#evaluation) that part of the fall in entry predates GenAI.

**Caveat.** Raw rates from a household survey, with no controls or standard errors; occupation-by-age cells are small, especially for coding jobs.

### Tadepalli (2026), "Efficiency vs Capability in the Intelligence Explosion"

GovAI, arXiv 2610.33447, 24 October 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33447). Theory and calibration: a task-based growth model of AI research with Monte Carlo simulations over the inputs.

**What it finds.** A self-sustaining acceleration of AI progress, with compute and human researchers fixed, needs both capability (the share of research tasks AI can do) and inference efficiency (research output per unit of compute) to improve fast enough, and neither can make up for the other. Returns must exceed one on both margins. Baseline returns are 1.07 for efficiency and 3.45 for capability, the latter resting on METR's 131-day doubling of time horizons. Both conditions hold in 49% of 200,000 draws; efficiency is the lower margin in 80%, and uncertainty about it explains 45% of the uncertainty about the outcome.

**Where it fits.** Replaces the scalar research-efficiency units of Eth and Davidson (2025) and Davidson and Houlden (2025) with tasks, and finds inference efficiency, not capability, is the likely constraint (companion review). It also turns the time-horizon trend the review uses (Kwa et al. 2025; [Main patterns](#main-patterns)) into a share of tasks automated per doubling.

**Caveat.** The 49% is not a probability: the inputs come from short series and a few open-weight models, and compute is held fixed, so this is a software-only explosion.

### Bianchini and Coroama (2026), "LLMs and the Organization of Scientific Work"

BETA, University of Strasbourg, arXiv 2611.02996, 2 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02996). Event study around ChatGPT's release against the 2018 to 2021 trend; every team was exposed at once, so no untreated group.

**What it finds.** Since ChatGPT, research teams spread each task across fewer members, and each member does a narrower set of tasks. In contribution statements for 117,211 PLOS ONE papers (2018 to 2025), the share of a team credited with ideation is 2.6 percentage points below its 2022 level by 2025, writing 2.7 points and coordination 1.4 points lower, reversing a rising pre-2022 trend. The change comes from newly formed teams, about 90% of papers; teams that had published together before show no significant break.

**Where it fits.** Extends Hoffmann et al. (2025), where a coding assistant moved developers away from coordinating others, to whole teams: one person covers inputs that used to need several (section [“Who holds knowledge?”](#who-holds-knowledge)). Reorganisation happens when teams form, not within existing ones, consistent with the inertia and reorganisation costs in Dillon et al. (2025) and Bergeaud and Bunel (2026) (section [“Does reorganisation create new tasks?”](#does-reorganisation-create-new-tasks)).

**Caveat.** Year comparisons cannot separate ChatGPT from other post-2022 changes, such as the end of pandemic disruption; statements record who took part, not how much.

### Gans and Landry (2026), "Training Your Replacement: Worker Incentives and the Pace of Automation"

NBER Working Paper 35950, 26 October 2026. [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35950). Theory: an efficiency wage model in which a worker trains the AI that replaces them; outside the causal standard, reported for the organisation section.

**What it finds.** When workers must train the AI that will replace them, firms pay them more and may automate more slowly than is efficient. Completing training ends the job, so dismissal is a weaker threat and the firm must raise the worker's rent. The worker obstructs whenever training is faster than obstruction is detected, so the firm trains below the fastest feasible rate; obstruction never occurs in equilibrium, yet it sets the pace. A credible completion bonus equal to the worker's rent without AI removes both the extra rent and the slowdown. No data; the motivating case is Meta's 2026 screen-recording programme, petitioned against by more than 1,600 employees.

**Where it fits.** Acemoglu et al. (2026) argue that rents push firms towards replacement automation; here, when the worker holds the knowledge the AI needs, rents rise and automation slows (section [“Organisation: who decides how AI is used”](#organisation-who-decides-how-ai-is-used)). It adds a channel to the slow diffusion the review explains by management and restructuring costs (Coyle et al. 2026; Bergeaud and Bunel 2026; [Main patterns](#main-patterns)), and predicts higher pay while workers train their replacement.

**Caveat.** Untested predictions; if the AI only augments the job, the wage premium and the slowdown vanish.

### Fernández-Villaverde (2026), "Saving for the Machines"

NBER Working Paper 35964, 2 November 2026. [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35964). Calibrated growth model with optimal household saving and a transition to full automation over 2025 to 2045; theory, for the companion growth review.

**What it finds.** How fast automation raises growth depends on how much households save, not only on the technology. In a US-calibrated task model where every task becomes automatable by 2045, growth with strongly complementary tasks reaches 11.7% a year under optimal saving, against 2.2% if saving stays at its 2025 rate of 23%. Saving rises because the capital share goes from one third to one. Under full automation wages stay between a quarter and a half of trend; if only 85% of tasks become automatable, the last human tasks earn scarcity rents and wages rise to nine times trend. Households with no assets lose 40% to 65% of their consumption; keeping their labour income on trend costs 4% to 20% of GDP at the peak.

**Where it fits.** Jones and Tonetti (2026) set $$\sigma=0.2$$ and find slow acceleration; with optimal saving the same weak links give double-digit growth, which locates the bottleneck in capital supply rather than technology (companion review). Gains go to capital, so who owns the machines decides who captures them ([Main patterns](#main-patterns)).

**Caveat.** Benchmarks, not forecasts: the automation path is assumed, and a representative household is the most elastic case for capital supply.

### Domnisoru and Heikkinen (2026), "How is AI Affecting College Major and Course Choices? Evidence from Finland"

RFBerlin Discussion Paper 293/26, 2 November 2026. [Paper](https://www.rfberlin.com/network-paper/how-is-ai-affecting-college-major-and-course-choices-evidence-from-finland/). Event studies on Finnish administrative data on university applications and courses, by the AI exposure of each degree; the authors call the application results not causal.

**What it finds.** Finnish students are turning away from AI-exposed university degrees, and the shift is larger than the job losses so far. Between the 2022 and 2026 rounds, first-choice applicants to ICT fell 38.0% while applicants to all other fields rose 16.5%. Within the same broad field, one standard deviation more exposure lowers first-choice applicants by 8.8% in 2025 and 14.0% in 2026, with pre-2023 coefficients indistinguishable from zero. By 2026, graduates of degrees one standard deviation more exposed had employment 1 point lower. Enrolled students do not switch degrees more.

**Where it fits.** Adjustment through entry now starts before the labour market, at the choice of degree, extending Orr et al. (2026) on graduates of exposed majors (section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry); [Main patterns](#main-patterns), $$\kappa_o$$). Hosseini and Lichtinger (2026) predict more entry into exposed occupations; applications fall instead, and students seem to act on expected, not current, conditions ($$\Delta\ln ps_o$$). Extending Kauhanen and Rouvinen (2025) to March 2026 still shows no economy-wide effect within firms.

**Caveat.** ICT is four degree codes in one field, so few clusters; post-pandemic shifts and a 2025 entrance exam reform could also move choices. ICT remains oversubscribed, so enrolment has not yet fallen.

### Levy Yeyati, Judzik and Barbiero (2026), "Practice Makes Perfect: Tacit Knowledge, Generative AI, and Returns to Schooling and Experience"

Universidad Torcuato Di Tella, arXiv, 26 October 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36008). Difference in differences on US Current Population Survey wages, most against least exposed occupations, before and after ChatGPT.

**What it finds.** In exposed occupations, ChatGPT lowered the wage return to schooling and raised the return to early career experience. On 655,919 earnings observations (2018 to June 2026), comparing the top and bottom quarters of the Felten, Raj and Seamans index, the return to a year of schooling falls by 0.55 log points from 7.5, about 7.3%. The return to a year of experience rises by 0.26 log points at three years, is unchanged at fifteen and falls by 0.12 at twenty-five. Pre-trends are flat and a pandemic-dated placebo shows no effect.

**Where it fits.** Extends Azar et al. (2026a), who find lower wages and a falling college premium in exposed occupations, to the price of schooling inside them: AI supplies codified knowledge, so schooling is worth less (section [“Exposure designs: wages”](#exposure-designs-wages)). Practice gains value early, as in Garicano and Rayo (2025) (section [“Why these patterns: theory”](#why-these-patterns-theory)), which contrasts with the field experiments where novices gain most (Brynjolfsson et al. 2025; [Main patterns](#main-patterns), $$\pi_o$$).

**Caveat.** Exposed occupations also faced remote work and the tech hiring slowdown; top coding hits 11.5% of exposed observations against 0.7% of others. Not yet peer reviewed.

### Hyman et al. (2025), "How Retrainable are AI-Exposed Workers?"

NBER Working Paper 34174, August 2025, revised May 2026. [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34174). Matched comparison of publicly funded trainees with job seekers who received only job search help; selection on observables.

**What it finds.** Public job training pays off for workers leaving AI-exposed jobs, mainly by moving them into less exposed occupations. Across 1.9 million training spells funded by the US Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (2012 to 2024), trainees earn about &#36;900 (from high-exposure occupations) to &#36;2,200 (from low-exposure ones) more per quarter than matched job seekers who received only job search help, in 2010 dollars. For trainees from high-exposure occupations, the gain rose from about &#36;900 a quarter before 2020 to &#36;2,900 by 2022 to 2024, mostly through moves into less exposed occupations. About 40 to 45% of occupations are "AI retrainable": their workers earn more after moving into more exposed ones.

**Where it fits.** First evidence on how displaced workers move, which the review marks as not yet observed (Manning and Aguirre 2026; [Main patterns](#main-patterns), $$\kappa_o$$): retrained workers leave exposed occupations with earnings gains. A smaller part of the gain comes from training into AI-complementary work, extending Humlum and Vestergaard (2025) (section [“Does reorganisation create new tasks?”](#does-reorganisation-create-new-tasks)).

**Caveat.** Matching cannot rule out more motivated trainees; occupation codes exist for only 22% of spells, and 2022 to 2024 was a very tight labour market.
