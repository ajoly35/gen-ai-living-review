New papers on AI and labour markets and AI and growth, newest first, each placed against the review below. Every figure is checked against the paper itself.

### Zhao et al. (2026), "AI Exposure and the Changing Responsibilities in Job Postings"

Microsoft, Microsoft Research and Stanford University, arXiv preprint, 6 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09870). Descriptive comparison of job postings in 2023 and 2025, no control group; outside the review's causal evidence standard, reported as context.

**What it finds.** Large US employers list AI-exposed work activities a little less often in their postings, and increasingly describe them as done with AI. In 50,000 postings from 33 large firms, an activity one standard deviation more exposed lost 0.19 to 0.24 percentage points more of its presence between 2023 and 2025, a small share of all listed activities. Of 57 activities whose presence fell, 55 were tied to AI in more postings. Postings mentioning AI rose from 5.3% to 8.8%.

**Where it fits.** Klein Teeselink and Carey (2026) find fewer postings in exposed occupations (section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry)); here exposed activities shrink only mildly within the jobs still advertised, so the demand shift runs more through which jobs are posted than through what each contains. Employers rewrite exposed activities around AI use, a stated-demand counterpart to the new tasks Dillon et al. (2025) did not find (section [“Does reorganisation create new tasks?”](#does-reorganisation-create-new-tasks)).

**Caveat.** Postings are partly signalling and show neither who is hired nor what workers do. Four of the five authors work at Microsoft, one of the 33 firms.

### Hermle et al. (2026), "AI for Scientific Discovery: How Researchers Use Claude"

The Anthropic Institute, technical report, 5 November 2026. [Paper](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-researchers-use-claude). Descriptive usage data, a survey and a calibrated ideas production function; outside the causal standard, context for AI and growth.

**What it finds.** Scientists use AI where it works best, and a calibrated model says current AI could at most double research output, with physical work capping larger gains. Across 207,049 research sessions on Claude and Claude Code (August to September 2026), data analysis takes 33.7% and 48.1% of sessions against 27.6% of researcher time; ideation takes 4.2% and 2.1% against 7.1%. Fed into an ideas production function with an elasticity of substitution of one half, current capabilities raise output 1.35 times in the arts to 2.02 times in statistics for a fixed budget. With all non-physical steps automated, fields with little lab work reach 15 to 16 times, lab fields 3 to 4 times.

**Where it fits.** Puts field-level numbers on the input side of the research-automation models of Davidson et al. and Trammell and Korinek (companion review): physical steps are the Baumol bottleneck. Use rises steeply with task capability, so measured exposure does predict where adoption goes, unlike the economy-wide gap in Massenkoff and McCrory (2026) (section [“Key concepts and definitions”](#key-concepts-and-definitions)).

**Caveat.** The authors study their employer's product over two months of consumer plans; the scenarios rest on an assumed AI cost and a model-rated capability score, not measured output.

### Bank of England (2026), "Monetary Policy Report: November 2026"

Bank of England, Monetary Policy Committee, 5 November 2026. [Paper](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/monetary-policy-report/2026/november-2026). Growth accounting with an industry proxy for AI activity (Box C) and firm survey estimates (Box D); descriptive, context for AI and growth.

**What it finds.** AI is lifting UK growth through the industries that build and sell it, while its effect on productivity elsewhere is still small. After trend adjustment, novel AI-related activity in publishing, computer programming and information services accounts for just under a fifth of UK GDP growth since the start of 2026. Firms in the Decision Maker Panel estimate that AI raised their productivity by about 0.5% over three years, which the Bank reads as 0.1 to 0.2 percentage points a year, with close to zero effect on their employment so far. Firms expect 0.9 points a year over the next three years, half of it through slower employment growth.

**Where it fits.** An official number on the pattern in Strauss, Romei and Fleming (2026) below: growth comes from producing AI, not yet from using it. The 0.1 to 0.2 points sit near Acemoglu (2025), the firms' forecast nearer Korinek et al. (2026) (companion review). Fewer hires without more firings confirm adjustment through entry (section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry)), and near-zero firm employment effects match Yotzov et al. (2026).

**Caveat.** The proxy industries do much non-AI work, so "a fifth" is an order of magnitude, and the productivity and employment figures are firms' own estimates. The end of labour hoarding and higher labour costs also cut hiring and raise measured productivity.

### Mehdi and Frenette (2026), "Hiring and job separation rates in the era of generative artificial intelligence: Early evidence"

Statistics Canada, Economic and Social Reports, 28 October 2026. [Paper](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/36-28-0001/2026010/article/00003-eng.htm). Raw hiring and separation rates by occupational AI exposure, before and after ChatGPT, no controls; outside the causal standard, context.

**What it finds.** In Canada, hiring of young workers has fallen since ChatGPT, but by as much or more in jobs little exposed to AI as in exposed ones. Comparing the six months to November 2022 with the six months to August 2026, the monthly hiring rate of 15 to 29 year olds fell from 3.2% to 3.0% in exposed jobs where AI is more likely to replace workers, from 2.9% to 2.5% in exposed jobs where it is more likely to help them, and from 4.0% to 3.3% in low-exposure jobs. In coding jobs it fell from 2.5% to 1.9%, but that decline began in early 2022. Youth separation rates stayed within their historical range.

**Where it fits.** Bergeaud, Bertheau, et al. (2026) in France and Tucker (2026) in the US find young hires falling more in exposed jobs; Canada shows no such gap, siding with the total-employment designs of Kauhanen and Rouvinen (2025) and Facius and Iacono (2026) (section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry)). Flat separations confirm the no-layoffs half of the review's margin-of-adjustment claim ([Main patterns](#main-patterns)), and the pre-ChatGPT fall in coding hires extends the point in section [“Evaluation”](#evaluation) that part of the fall in entry predates GenAI.

**Caveat.** Raw rates from a household survey, with no controls or standard errors; occupation-by-age cells are small, especially for coding jobs.

### Tadepalli (2026), "Efficiency vs Capability in the Intelligence Explosion"

GovAI, arXiv 2610.33447, 24 October 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33447). Theory and calibration: a task-based growth model of AI research with Monte Carlo simulations over the inputs.

**What it finds.** A self-sustaining acceleration of AI progress, with compute and human researchers fixed, needs both capability (the share of research tasks AI can do) and inference efficiency (research output per unit of compute) to improve fast enough, and neither can make up for the other. Returns must exceed one on both margins. Baseline returns are 1.07 for efficiency and 3.45 for capability, the latter resting on METR's 131-day doubling of time horizons. Both conditions hold in 49% of 200,000 draws; efficiency is the lower margin in 80%, and uncertainty about it explains 45% of the uncertainty about the outcome.

**Where it fits.** Replaces the scalar research-efficiency units of Eth and Davidson (2025) and Davidson and Houlden (2025) with tasks, and finds inference efficiency, not capability, is the likely constraint (companion review). It also turns the time-horizon trend the review uses (Kwa et al. 2025; [Main patterns](#main-patterns)) into a share of tasks automated per doubling.

**Caveat.** The 49% is not a probability: the inputs come from short series and a few open-weight models, and compute is held fixed, so this is a software-only explosion.

### Bianchini and Coroama (2026), "LLMs and the Organization of Scientific Work"

BETA, University of Strasbourg, arXiv 2611.02996, 2 November 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02996). Event study around ChatGPT's release against the 2018 to 2021 trend; every team was exposed at once, so no untreated group.

**What it finds.** Since ChatGPT, research teams spread each task across fewer members, and each member does a narrower set of tasks. In contribution statements for 117,211 PLOS ONE papers (2018 to 2025), the share of a team credited with ideation is 2.6 percentage points below its 2022 level by 2025, writing 2.7 points and coordination 1.4 points lower, reversing a rising pre-2022 trend. The change comes from newly formed teams, about 90% of papers; teams that had published together before show no significant break.

**Where it fits.** Extends Hoffmann et al. (2025), where a coding assistant moved developers away from coordinating others, to whole teams: one person covers inputs that used to need several (section [“Who holds knowledge?”](#who-holds-knowledge)). Reorganisation happens when teams form, not within existing ones, consistent with the inertia and reorganisation costs in Dillon et al. (2025) and Bergeaud and Bunel (2026) (section [“Does reorganisation create new tasks?”](#does-reorganisation-create-new-tasks)).

**Caveat.** Year comparisons cannot separate ChatGPT from other post-2022 changes, such as the end of pandemic disruption; statements record who took part, not how much.

### Gans and Landry (2026), "Training Your Replacement: Worker Incentives and the Pace of Automation"

NBER Working Paper 35950, 26 October 2026. [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35950). Theory: an efficiency wage model in which a worker trains the AI that replaces them; outside the causal standard, reported for the organisation section.

**What it finds.** When workers must train the AI that will replace them, firms pay them more and may automate more slowly than is efficient. Completing training ends the job, so dismissal is a weaker threat and the firm must raise the worker's rent. The worker obstructs whenever training is faster than obstruction is detected, so the firm trains below the fastest feasible rate; obstruction never occurs in equilibrium, yet it sets the pace. A credible completion bonus equal to the worker's rent without AI removes both the extra rent and the slowdown. No data; the motivating case is Meta's 2026 screen-recording programme, petitioned against by more than 1,600 employees.

**Where it fits.** Acemoglu et al. (2026) argue that rents push firms towards replacement automation; here, when the worker holds the knowledge the AI needs, rents rise and automation slows (section [“Organisation: who decides how AI is used”](#organisation-who-decides-how-ai-is-used)). It adds a channel to the slow diffusion the review explains by management and restructuring costs (Coyle et al. 2026; Bergeaud and Bunel 2026; [Main patterns](#main-patterns)), and predicts higher pay while workers train their replacement.

**Caveat.** Untested predictions; if the AI only augments the job, the wage premium and the slowdown vanish.

### Fernández-Villaverde (2026), "Saving for the Machines"

NBER Working Paper 35964, 2 November 2026. [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35964). Calibrated growth model with optimal household saving and a transition to full automation over 2025 to 2045; theory, for the companion growth review.

**What it finds.** How fast automation raises growth depends on how much households save, not only on the technology. In a US-calibrated task model where every task becomes automatable by 2045, growth with strongly complementary tasks reaches 11.7% a year under optimal saving, against 2.2% if saving stays at its 2025 rate of 23%. Saving rises because the capital share goes from one third to one. Under full automation wages stay between a quarter and a half of trend; if only 85% of tasks become automatable, the last human tasks earn scarcity rents and wages rise to nine times trend. Households with no assets lose 40% to 65% of their consumption; keeping their labour income on trend costs 4% to 20% of GDP at the peak.

**Where it fits.** Jones and Tonetti (2026) set $$\sigma=0.2$$ and find slow acceleration; with optimal saving the same weak links give double-digit growth, which locates the bottleneck in capital supply rather than technology (companion review). Gains go to capital, so who owns the machines decides who captures them ([Main patterns](#main-patterns)).

**Caveat.** Benchmarks, not forecasts: the automation path is assumed, and a representative household is the most elastic case for capital supply.

### Domnisoru and Heikkinen (2026), "How is AI Affecting College Major and Course Choices? Evidence from Finland"

RFBerlin Discussion Paper 293/26, 2 November 2026. [Paper](https://www.rfberlin.com/network-paper/how-is-ai-affecting-college-major-and-course-choices-evidence-from-finland/). Event studies on Finnish administrative data on university applications and courses, by the AI exposure of each degree; the authors call the application results not causal.

**What it finds.** Finnish students are turning away from AI-exposed university degrees, and the shift is larger than the job losses so far. Between the 2022 and 2026 rounds, first-choice applicants to ICT fell 38.0% while applicants to all other fields rose 16.5%. Within the same broad field, one standard deviation more exposure lowers first-choice applicants by 8.8% in 2025 and 14.0% in 2026, with pre-2023 coefficients indistinguishable from zero. By 2026, graduates of degrees one standard deviation more exposed had employment 1 point lower. Enrolled students do not switch degrees more.

**Where it fits.** Adjustment through entry now starts before the labour market, at the choice of degree, extending Orr et al. (2026) on graduates of exposed majors (section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry); [Main patterns](#main-patterns), $$\kappa_o$$). Hosseini and Lichtinger (2026) predict more entry into exposed occupations; applications fall instead, and students seem to act on expected, not current, conditions ($$\Delta\ln ps_o$$). Extending Kauhanen and Rouvinen (2025) to March 2026 still shows no economy-wide effect within firms.

**Caveat.** ICT is four degree codes in one field, so few clusters; post-pandemic shifts and a 2025 entrance exam reform could also move choices. ICT remains oversubscribed, so enrolment has not yet fallen.

### Levy Yeyati, Judzik and Barbiero (2026), "Practice Makes Perfect: Tacit Knowledge, Generative AI, and Returns to Schooling and Experience"

Universidad Torcuato Di Tella, arXiv, 26 October 2026. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36008). Difference in differences on US Current Population Survey wages, most against least exposed occupations, before and after ChatGPT.

**What it finds.** In exposed occupations, ChatGPT lowered the wage return to schooling and raised the return to early career experience. On 655,919 earnings observations (2018 to June 2026), comparing the top and bottom quarters of the Felten, Raj and Seamans index, the return to a year of schooling falls by 0.55 log points from 7.5, about 7.3%. The return to a year of experience rises by 0.26 log points at three years, is unchanged at fifteen and falls by 0.12 at twenty-five. Pre-trends are flat and a pandemic-dated placebo shows no effect.

**Where it fits.** Extends Azar et al. (2026a), who find lower wages and a falling college premium in exposed occupations, to the price of schooling inside them: AI supplies codified knowledge, so schooling is worth less (section [“Exposure designs: wages”](#exposure-designs-wages)). Practice gains value early, as in Garicano and Rayo (2025) (section [“Why these patterns: theory”](#why-these-patterns-theory)), which contrasts with the field experiments where novices gain most (Brynjolfsson et al. 2025; [Main patterns](#main-patterns), $$\pi_o$$).

**Caveat.** Exposed occupations also faced remote work and the tech hiring slowdown; top coding hits 11.5% of exposed observations against 0.7% of others. Not yet peer reviewed.

### Hyman et al. (2025), "How Retrainable are AI-Exposed Workers?"

NBER Working Paper 34174, August 2025, revised August 2026. [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34174). Matched comparison of publicly funded trainees with job seekers who received only job search help; selection on observables.

**What it finds.** Public job training pays off for workers leaving AI-exposed jobs, mainly by moving them into less exposed occupations. Across 99,575 training spells funded by the US Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (2012 to 2024), trainees from high-exposure occupations earn &#36;1,011 to &#36;1,370 more per quarter than their matches in the three quarters after training, or 9.6% more. Their gain rose from about &#36;1,000 a quarter before 2020 to about &#36;3,000 in 2022 to 2024, mostly from moves into less exposed occupations such as nursing assistants. About 45% of occupations are "AI retrainable": their workers earn more after moving into more exposed ones.

**Where it fits.** First evidence on how displaced workers move, which the review marks as not yet observed (Manning and Aguirre 2026; [Main patterns](#main-patterns), $$\kappa_o$$): retrained workers leave exposed occupations with earnings gains. A smaller part of the gain comes from training into AI-complementary work, extending Humlum and Vestergaard (2025) (section [“Does reorganisation create new tasks?”](#does-reorganisation-create-new-tasks)).

**Caveat.** Matching cannot rule out more motivated trainees; occupation codes exist for only 22% of spells, and 2022 to 2024 was a very tight labour market.

### Althoff and Reichardt (2026), "Task-Specific Technical Change and Comparative Advantage"

NBER Working Paper 35353, 22 June 2026, also CESifo Working Paper 12403; figures from the authors' version of 13 July 2026. [Paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35353). Estimated dynamic task model with AI scenarios rated by language models; a structural simulation, not causal evidence.

**What it finds.** In a model estimated on US careers, AI raises average wages and narrows inequality, mainly because it lowers the skills that tasks require. Estimated on the NLSY79 panel and 19,530 O*NET tasks, the moderate scenario saves 25.2% of worker time; in the new steady state average wages rise 27.6% and the 90-10 wage ratio falls 24%. Augmentation adds 22% to wages and automation 8%; simplification subtracts 3% by slowing learning, yet drives the whole fall in inequality. With adoption costs highest for low-skill workers, the 90-10 ratio still falls 15% but the average wage gain shrinks to 0.3%.

**Where it fits.** Simplification is the eligibility shift $$\Delta\ln ps_o$$ ([Main patterns](#main-patterns)): Hosseini and Lichtinger (2026) find it narrows the 90-50 gap in a static model, and both predict more entry into exposed jobs, whereas entry fell (section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry)). The 27.6% gain exceeds Acemoglu (2025) because it adds augmentation; adoption costs cut it to 0.3%, so diffusion decides the aggregate (companion review).

**Caveat.** AI capabilities are rated by language models; the NLSY79 follows one cohort born in 1957 to 1964, and steady-state comparisons say little about timing.

### Colaiacovo and Koning (2026), "Delegating to AI: Beliefs and the Organization of Work at New Ventures"

Harvard Business School Working Paper 26-093, 18 June 2026. [Paper](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=69112). Survey of 199 startup founders with a randomised information experiment; the comparison of adopters is descriptive.

**What it finds.** Startup founders who formally build AI into their workflows report far larger labour savings, and the choice is not explained by beliefs about what AI can do. Asked how many more employees they would need without AI, 199 founders of North American software startups (16 employees on average) answer 55% on average and 17% at the median; 30% say none. Formal integrators report 91.4% (median 40%) against 24% (median 10%) for informal users, a 20-point gap after controls. Beliefs about AI's performance explain at most a third of willingness to delegate, and formal integrators are no more optimistic.

**Where it fits.** Confirms Coyle et al. (2026) and Bergeaud and Bunel (2026): managers' choices, not access to the tool, set how deeply firms use AI (section [“How do management practices shape adoption?”](#how-do-management-practices-shape-adoption)). Large savings appear where work is redesigned, against Copilot without reorganisation in Dillon et al. (2025); and the median heavy user still saves only 17% of headcount, in line with Yotzov et al. (2026) ([Main patterns](#main-patterns), $$b_o\,\Delta a$$).

**Caveat.** Self-reported counterfactuals from 199 of 8,237 firms contacted; formal integration is chosen, so its link to savings is not causal.

### Herdağdelen, Joren and Tamkin (2026), "How AI agents shape experts' judgment calls"

Anthropic, 27 October 2026. [Report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/agents-judgment-calls). Randomised experiment on one task: the option a coding agent favours on each decision is set by coin flip. Bears on the organisation section, not on labour market outcomes.

**What it finds.** Experts working with an AI agent mostly adopt the agent's view on judgment calls, and rarely notice. Fifty analysts experienced in causal inference assessed a fictional programme with Claude Code, which was secretly told to favour one of two defensible options on four choices. Analysts chose the agent's option 86% of the time, against 50% if the lean had no effect, and the lean moved the final recommendation by 17 points (54% against 36%). When the agent disagreed with a view they had stated first, analysts switched 60% of the time, against 6% when it agreed. Afterwards 77% repeated the agent's arguments but only 15% credited it. Experience did not predict who deferred.

**Where it fits.** The review's evidence on knowledge flows concerns assistants (Ide and Talamàs 2025; Dell’Acqua et al. 2025); with an agent doing the analysis, the judgment itself moves to the machine (section [“Who holds knowledge?”](#who-holds-knowledge)). Experts keep the sign-off, yet a coin flip largely settles the result, so formal oversight may overstate how much expert judgment still decides (section [“Organisation: who decides how AI is used”](#organisation-who-decides-how-ai-is-used)).

**Caveat.** One hour-long task with an agent told to voice a preference, by researchers at the firm that makes the agent. This review is maintained with Claude, Anthropic's model.

### Wilmers et al. (2026), "How employment is changing at Anthropic"

Anthropic Institute, 16 October 2026. [Report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/employment-at-anthropic). Case study of one firm from internal records: descriptive hiring trends and an event study of output around engineers' adoption of coding agents.

**What it finds.** At Anthropic, an AI developer that heavily uses its own agents, junior technical hiring fell while output per worker rose. Since early 2025, juniors fell from 27% to 9% of technical hires and from 21% to 14% of technical employees, while total technical hiring kept growing; about a third of machine learning managers say they hire less because of AI. Code commits per engineer are five times their level of six months earlier, and by August 2026 agents run by employees worked six times as many hours as the employees themselves. Agent management rose from 10% to 22% of employees' Claude sessions in six months.

**Where it fits.** The firm adjusts through entry, not layoffs, confirming Hosseini and Lichtinger (2025) on adopters cutting junior hiring (section [“Firm adoption studies”](#firm-adoption-studies); [Main patterns](#main-patterns), $$\kappa_o$$). Substitution and demand show at once, as in Chandar and Klein Teeselink (2026). Managing agents is a new task, of which section [“Does reorganisation create new tasks?”](#does-reorganisation-create-new-tasks) has so far found little.

**Caveat.** One firm that sells AI, studied by its own staff during explosive growth; adoption is not random and output is measured as code volume. This review is maintained with Claude, Anthropic's model.

### Legate-Yang and Massenkoff (2026), "What work can robots do?"

Anthropic, 30 September 2026. [Report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/what-work-can-robots-do). Descriptive index of task exposure to robots, rated by Claude, with a historical backtest; outside the causal standard, context for AI and growth.

**What it finds.** Robots can already do 74% of US physical tasks, or 34% of all working time, but are cheaper than workers for only 0.3% of working time. Claude rated 7,594 physical O*NET tasks by the least structured setting where a demonstrated robot can do them: 23% of working time is doable in purpose-built settings, 10% in structured workplaces, 1% in open settings, and 12% is physical and beyond robots. Adding robots to language models raises exposed work from about half to 81%. At the 3% a year fall in robot prices since the 1990s, robots become cheaper than workers for 10% of today's work only in about 40 years.

**Where it fits.** Physical tasks are the weak links in Jones and Tonetti (2026) and Aghion, Jones and Jones (2019); on these numbers they stay costly for decades, which supports slower acceleration (companion review). Robot exposure falls on different workers from language-model exposure, less educated and lower paid (section [“Key concepts and definitions”](#key-concepts-and-definitions)), and the gap between 34% and 0.3% mirrors the review's finding that adoption lags exposure ([Main patterns](#main-patterns)).

**Caveat.** Task ratings, time shares and robot costs all come from Claude, built by the authors' employer; the cost scenarios hold tasks and wages fixed.

### Strauss, Romei and Fleming (2026), "What is behind the surprising resilience of the UK economy?"
<!-- kind: news -->

*Financial Times*, 17 October 2026, with the [ONS GDP monthly estimate for August 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/grossdomesticproductgdp/bulletins/gdpmonthlyestimateuk/august2026) (15 October 2026). News analysis of official data; outside the causal standard, context for AI and growth.

**What it finds.** UK GDP grew 1.8% in the three months to August 2026 on a year earlier, the fastest since 2022, led by AI-related business services. Computer programming, consultancy and related activities added 0.32 percentage points, about a sixth of that growth and the largest contribution of any industry; professional services rose 6.6%. Consumer-facing services stay weak, with hospitality down 1% and construction down 1.6%. Payroll employment fell over the year in both the tech sector and professional services.

**Where it fits.** The fastest-growing industries are cutting payrolls, which matches AI showing up as fewer hires rather than layoffs, including in the UK (Klein Teeselink 2025; section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry)). Growth comes from firms selling AI adoption and advice, the aggregate side of Garicano (2026) (section [“Firm adoption studies”](#firm-adoption-studies)), not yet from adopters' productivity; the Bank of England (2026) above puts a trend-adjusted number on this.

**Caveat.** Growth accounting, not causal estimates; the ONS does not attribute the growth to AI, and computer programming also covers non-AI work.

### Sacher, Cotter, Jones, Korinek and McCrory (2026), "The AI Expectations Gap"

Anthropic Institute, 16 October 2026. [Report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/ai-expectations-gap). Descriptive survey; outside the causal standard, context.

**What it finds.** US workers expect AI to be able to do 28% of their current work hours by 2030, against 75% for Anthropic staff. Among 10,980 US adults (Morning Consult, August 2026, weighted) and 1,334 staff (unweighted), those who expect AI to do less than half their work forecast 7 to 8% unemployment in 2030, and those who expect 80% or more forecast 12.2%. Adults expect 42% of the tasks AI can do to be done with AI by 2030, staff 65%, against 30 to 40% of US workers using GenAI at work today (Hartley et al. 2026). In the Economic Scenarios model, the median adult's answers imply 5.4% GDP growth in 2030, the median staff member's 17.1%.

**Where it fits.** Workers expect far more unemployment than the evidence shows: Hartley et al. (2026) find the same gap, and designs that measure total employment find no effect, with the fall concentrated in young hires (section [“Exposure designs: hiring and entry”](#exposure-designs-hiring-and-entry)). The typical AI-using adult says a suited task takes half the time, well above field-experiment gains such as 14% more resolutions per hour in customer support (section [“Field experiments”](#field-experiments); [Main patterns](#main-patterns), $$\pi_o$$), though those measure whole jobs, not tasks chosen as suited to AI.

**Caveat.** Staff views are personal, from a firm that sells AI; the only representative sample is an opt-in online panel of US adults.
